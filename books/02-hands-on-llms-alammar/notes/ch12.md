# A12 — Fine-Tuning Generation Models

> **Thesis:** You can adapt a large language model on one consumer GPU, because you do not have to
> train most of it — and the two ideas that make that true, LoRA and quantization, are worth
> understanding precisely.

## Why this chapter matters

This is where LLM fine-tuning stops being a thing only labs do. LoRA and QLoRA are the techniques
that put a 7B fine-tune on a 16 GB card, and the reasoning behind them is genuinely elegant.

Read it alongside [H7](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch07.md), which supplies the memory
arithmetic and — more importantly — the argument about *whether you should fine-tune at all*.
Alammar tells you how; Huyen tells you when.

## Core concepts

### The two stages of post-training

1. **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)** — train on (instruction, response) pairs. Teaches the model
   the *format and behaviour* of following instructions. This is what
   [R7](../../03-build-llm-from-scratch-raschka/notes/ch07.md) has you implement by hand.
2. **Preference tuning** — teach the model which of two valid responses humans prefer. RLHF via
   PPO ([G18](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch18.md)), or **DPO**, which optimizes the
   preference objective directly with no reward model and no RL loop — vastly simpler, and now
   the common choice.

SFT teaches *what to do*; preference tuning teaches *how to do it well*. A model that has had only
SFT follows instructions but is often verbose, hedging, or subtly unhelpful.

### Why full fine-tuning is expensive

Full fine-tuning of a 7B model in fp16 needs roughly:

| Component | Memory |
|---|---|
| Weights (fp16) | 14 GB |
| Gradients (fp16) | 14 GB |
| Adam optimizer states (fp32 m and v) | ~56 GB |
| Activations | Varies with batch and sequence |
| **Total** | **~90 GB+** |

The optimizer states dominate — Adam keeps two fp32 moments per parameter. That is the number
that puts full fine-tuning out of reach, and it is why the techniques below target *trainable
parameter count* specifically.

### LoRA — Low-Rank Adaptation

The observation: the *update* to a weight matrix during fine-tuning has low intrinsic rank. You
are not teaching the model language again; you are nudging it.

So freeze W and learn a low-rank decomposition of the update:

$$W' = W + \Delta W = W + BA$$

where $W$ is $d \times k$, $B$ is $d \times r$, $A$ is $r \times k$, and **r ≪ min(d, k)**.

For a 4096 × 4096 matrix with r = 8: 16.7M parameters become 65k. **Roughly 0.4%.**

Consequences that matter:

- Gradients and optimizer states exist only for A and B, so the 56 GB of Adam state collapses to
  megabytes.
- **Adapters are tiny** — a LoRA is often a few MB. You can keep dozens for different tasks and
  swap them against one frozen base model.
- **Zero inference overhead if you merge.** $W + BA$ can be folded back into $W$ after training.
- A is initialized randomly and **B is initialized to zero**, so $BA = 0$ at the start and
  training begins exactly at the base model's behaviour.

Key hyperparameters:

- **r** — the rank. 8–64 typical. Higher means more capacity and more memory.
- **alpha** — a scaling factor; the update is scaled by α/r. A common convention is α = 2r.
- **target_modules** — which matrices get adapters. Attention projections are standard; including
  the FFN layers helps more but costs more.

### QLoRA — quantization on top

Combine LoRA with a **4-bit quantized frozen base model**:

- The base weights are stored in 4-bit **NF4** (a normal-float format matched to the roughly
  Gaussian distribution of neural network weights).
- Computation happens in bf16 — weights are dequantized on the fly, per block.
- **Double quantization** quantizes the quantization constants themselves.
- **Paged optimizers** spill optimizer state to CPU memory during spikes.

Result: **a 7B model fine-tunable on a single 16 GB GPU**, and 65B on a single 48 GB card. That is
the number that democratized fine-tuning.

The tradeoff is honest: slower per step (dequantization costs time), and a small quality cost
versus fp16 LoRA. Usually worth it.

### The data is the hard part

Alammar's practical emphasis, and it is correct: **format consistency and quality matter more
than volume.** A thousand well-curated, consistently-formatted examples beat ten thousand scraped
ones.

- Use the **exact chat template** the base model expects. A mismatch here is the single most
  common cause of a fine-tune that "did nothing."
- **Mask the loss on the prompt tokens.** You want the model to learn to produce *responses*, not
  to reproduce instructions. Most trainers do this via a completion-only collator; verify yours
  does.
- Diversity across the input distribution matters more than count.

[H8](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch08.md) treats dataset engineering as its own discipline,
and it deserves to be.

### DPO

Direct Preference Optimization skips the reward model entirely. Given (prompt, chosen, rejected)
triples, it directly optimizes a loss that increases the relative likelihood of the chosen
response, with a KL-style constraint against a frozen reference model to prevent drift.

Compared to PPO-based RLHF: no reward model to train, no RL loop to stabilize, far less compute,
much easier to get working. It is what most open-source post-training now uses.

## Key code

```python
from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model, prepare_model_for_kbit_training
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, BitsAndBytesConfig
import torch

# QLoRA: 4-bit frozen base + trainable low-rank adapters
bnb = BitsAndBytesConfig(
    load_in_4bit=True,
    bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4",              # normal-float, matched to weight distribution
    bnb_4bit_compute_dtype=torch.bfloat16,  # compute in bf16, store in 4-bit
    bnb_4bit_use_double_quant=True,
)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(model_id, quantization_config=bnb,
                                             device_map="auto")
model = prepare_model_for_kbit_training(model)

lora = LoraConfig(
    r=16, lora_alpha=32, lora_dropout=0.05,
    target_modules=["q_proj", "k_proj", "v_proj", "o_proj"],
    task_type="CAUSAL_LM",
)
model = get_peft_model(model, lora)
model.print_trainable_parameters()   # typically ~0.1-1% of total
```

```python
# Always use the model's own chat template — hand-formatting silently costs quality
text = tokenizer.apply_chat_template(
    [{"role": "user", "content": instruction},
     {"role": "assistant", "content": response}],
    tokenize=False,
)
```

## Gotchas

- **Wrong chat template.** The most common cause of a fine-tune that appears to do nothing. Use
  `apply_chat_template`.
- **Not masking prompt tokens in the loss.** The model learns to echo instructions. Use a
  completion-only collator.
- **Rank too high for the data.** Large r on 500 examples overfits. Start at 8–16.
- **Forgetting `prepare_model_for_kbit_training`.** Needed for gradient checkpointing and layer
  norm casting with quantized bases.
- **Fine-tuning to add knowledge.** Fine-tuning teaches *behaviour and format*; RAG supplies
  *facts*. Trying to inject facts by fine-tuning is the classic expensive mistake — see
  [H7](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch07.md).
- **No evaluation set.** You cannot tell improvement from catastrophic forgetting without one.
- **Merging LoRA into a quantized base.** Merge into the fp16 base, not the 4-bit one, or you
  compound quantization error.
- **Skipping the prompt-engineering baseline.** Try prompting first. Always.

## Exercises

**Understand**
1. Why do Adam's optimizer states dominate full fine-tuning memory?
2. Explain LoRA's low-rank decomposition and compute the parameter saving for a 4096×4096 matrix
   at r=8.
3. Why is B initialized to zero?
4. What does QLoRA add on top of LoRA, and what does it cost?
5. Why does DPO not need a reward model?

**Build**
1. QLoRA fine-tune a small model (1–3B) on an instruction dataset. Track GPU memory throughout —
   watching it stay under your card's limit is the point.
2. Sweep r over [4, 8, 16, 64] on a fixed dataset. Plot quality against trainable parameters and
   find the knee.
3. Fine-tune once with the correct chat template and once with a hand-written prompt format.
   Compare. The gap is why this gotcha is listed first.
4. Run DPO on preference pairs after SFT and evaluate both. Note what changes — usually tone and
   verbosity more than correctness.
5. Merge a LoRA adapter into the base model and verify inference speed matches the unadapted base.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does full fine-tuning of a 7B model need ~90 GB?
A: 14 GB weights (fp16) + 14 GB gradients + ~56 GB Adam optimizer states (two fp32 moments per parameter) + activations. The optimizer states dominate, which is why PEFT methods target trainable parameter count specifically.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is LoRA's core idea?
A: The weight *update* during fine-tuning has low intrinsic rank, so freeze W and learn W' = W + BA where B is d×r, A is r×k, and r ≪ min(d,k). For a 4096×4096 matrix at r=8, 16.7M parameters become 65k — about 0.4%.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why is LoRA's B matrix initialized to zero?
A: So that BA = 0 at initialization and the adapted model starts out behaving exactly like the base model. A is initialized randomly to break symmetry; if both were zero, nothing would ever learn.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What does QLoRA add, and what does it cost?
A: A 4-bit NF4-quantized frozen base with bf16 compute, double quantization, and paged optimizers — putting a 7B fine-tune on a 16 GB GPU. It costs some speed (on-the-fly dequantization) and a small amount of quality versus fp16 LoRA.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is DPO and why did it displace PPO-based RLHF for most teams?
A: Direct Preference Optimization trains on (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples with a loss that raises the chosen response's relative likelihood, constrained against a frozen reference model. No reward model, no RL loop — far simpler and cheaper to get working.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why must you mask the loss on prompt tokens during SFT?
A: Otherwise the model is trained to reproduce instructions as well as responses. You want it to learn to *generate the completion*, so loss should apply only to response tokens.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Should you fine-tune to add new factual knowledge?
A: No. Fine-tuning teaches behaviour, format, and style; RAG supplies facts. Injecting knowledge by fine-tuning is expensive, unreliable, hard to update, and gives you no citations.
<!-- /card -->
