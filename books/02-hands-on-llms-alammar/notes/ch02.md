# A2 — Tokens and Embeddings

> **Thesis:** An LLM never sees text. It sees integers, which it immediately converts into vectors
> — and almost every strange behaviour you will ever debug traces back to one of those two steps.

## Why this chapter matters

Tokenization is the most under-appreciated source of LLM weirdness. Why can't it count the r's in
"strawberry"? Why does it fail at arithmetic on long numbers? Why does the same prompt cost more
in Thai than in English? All tokenization. And embeddings are the substrate everything else runs
on — retrieval, clustering, classification, and the model's own internals.

You will implement all of this in [R2](../../03-build-llm-from-scratch-raschka/notes/ch02.md).
Here you build the intuition.

## Core concepts

### Tokenization

Text → integers. A **tokenizer** has a fixed **vocabulary** mapping strings to IDs.

Four granularities, with a real tradeoff:

| Level | Vocabulary | Sequence length | OOV problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Word** | Huge (100k+) | Short | Severe — unknown words are lost |
| **Subword** | ~30–100k | Moderate | **None** — decompose into known pieces |
| **Character** | Tiny (~100) | Very long | None |
| **Byte** | 256 | Longest | None — any input encodable |

**Subword wins** and every modern model uses it. It gets a manageable vocabulary, reasonable
sequence lengths, and — crucially — **no out-of-vocabulary problem**, because any unknown word
decomposes into known subwords. Contrast the oov-bucket hashing hack from
[G13](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch13.md).

**Byte Pair Encoding (BPE)**, the dominant algorithm, is beautifully simple:

1. Start with a vocabulary of individual characters (or bytes).
2. Count all adjacent pairs in the corpus; merge the most frequent into a new token.
3. Repeat until you reach the target vocabulary size.

Frequent words end up as single tokens; rare words remain split. The vocabulary is *learned from
the corpus*, which is why domain matters — a tokenizer trained on web text splits medical
terminology badly.

### Why tokenizers explain so much LLM behaviour

This is the payoff section. Keep these:

- **Character counting fails** because the model sees `["straw", "berry"]`, not letters. Asking
  it to count "r"s asks it to introspect a representation it does not have.
- **Arithmetic degrades on long numbers** because digit grouping is inconsistent — `1234` may be
  one token while `12345` is two, split unpredictably.
- **Non-English text costs more.** A tokenizer trained mostly on English fragments other
  languages into many more tokens. The same sentence in Thai or Hindi can cost 3–5× more tokens
  than in English — literally a higher price for the same meaning.
- **Whitespace matters.** In most BPE tokenizers `" the"` (with a leading space) and `"the"` are
  *different tokens*. Trailing whitespace in a prompt can measurably change output.
- **Code tokenizes differently.** Models trained on code have vocabulary entries for common
  indentation patterns and keywords.

### Comparing tokenizers

Alammar's side-by-side of BERT, GPT-2, GPT-4, Phi-3, and others on the same text is the best
exercise in the chapter. What differs:

- **Vocabulary size** — bigger means shorter sequences but a larger embedding matrix.
- **Special tokens** — `[CLS]`, `[SEP]`, `<|endoftext|>`, chat role markers. Different models,
  different conventions.
- **Case handling** — some lowercase everything (losing information), some do not.
- **Whitespace and code handling** — GPT-4's tokenizer handles indentation notably better than
  GPT-2's, which matters enormously for code.

### Token embeddings

After tokenization, each ID indexes into an **embedding matrix** of shape
`(vocab_size, embedding_dim)`. GPT-2 small: `(50257, 768)`. This is precisely the
`Embedding` layer from [G13](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch13.md) — a trainable lookup table,
learned by gradient descent along with everything else.

**Static vs contextual — the key distinction:**

- The **embedding matrix** holds one static vector per token. `embedding[token_id]` is
  context-free.
- After passing through the transformer's layers, each position holds a **contextual** vector
  incorporating information from surrounding tokens.

So "bank" enters with one vector and leaves each layer with a vector shaped by its neighbours.
**Both exist in the same model.** Confusing them is a common source of muddle.

### Text embeddings

To embed a *sentence* or *document*, you must reduce many token vectors to one. Options:

- **Mean pooling** — average the token vectors. Simple, surprisingly strong.
- **[CLS] token** — use BERT's special first token. Works only if the model was trained for it.
- **Trained pooling** — as in sentence-transformers, where the pooling is optimized for the task.

**Critically:** a raw LLM's token embeddings are *not* good sentence embeddings. Models like
`sentence-transformers` are specifically trained with a contrastive objective to make cosine
similarity meaningful ([A10](ch10.md)). Using mean-pooled GPT-2 vectors for retrieval will work
poorly, and people try it constantly.

### word2vec and contrastive training

The mechanism worth understanding because it recurs everywhere:

**skip-gram with negative sampling.** Take a word and a nearby word — a positive pair. Sample
random words as negatives. Train a classifier to distinguish real neighbours from random ones. The
embeddings are a by-product of that classification task.

**The negatives are essential.** Without them, the model trivially outputs "yes, related" for
everything and all vectors collapse to a single point. The contrast between positives and
negatives is what creates structure.

> This exact recipe reappears in [A10](ch10.md) for training text embedding models, and it is the
> engine behind CLIP's image–text alignment ([A9](ch09.md)) and modern retrieval models. Learn it
> once here.

**Embeddings beyond words.** Alammar's music recommendation example is a good demonstration:
treat playlists as "sentences" and songs as "words," run word2vec, and songs that co-occur in
playlists get similar vectors. **Anything with co-occurrence structure can be embedded** — users,
products, sessions, graph nodes.

## Key code

```python
from transformers import AutoTokenizer

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2")

text = "Understanding tokenization is underrated."
ids = tok(text).input_ids
for i in ids:
    print(f"{i:>6}  {tok.decode([i])!r}")   # print the pieces — do this often

# The leading-space trap: these are DIFFERENT tokens
print(tok(" the").input_ids, tok("the").input_ids)

# The embedding matrix: a plain lookup table
import torch
from transformers import AutoModel
model = AutoModel.from_pretrained("gpt2")
emb = model.get_input_embeddings().weight       # (50257, 768)
print(emb.shape)

# Static (context-free) vs contextual
static = emb[ids[0]]                              # one row of the table
contextual = model(torch.tensor([ids])).last_hidden_state[0, 0]  # after the layers
```

## Gotchas

- **Counting characters or reversing strings.** The model cannot see inside tokens. This is not
  fixable by prompting.
- **Assuming a token is a word.** Roughly 0.75 words per token in English, far worse in other
  languages. Budget context accordingly.
- **Trailing whitespace in a prompt.** Changes tokenization and can degrade output. Strip it.
- **Mixing tokenizer and model.** Always load them from the same checkpoint. A mismatch produces
  fluent nonsense rather than an error.
- **Using mean-pooled LLM embeddings for semantic search.** Use a model trained for embeddings.
- **Forgetting special tokens.** `add_special_tokens=False` when you should not, or vice versa,
  shifts everything.
- **Comparing embeddings across models.** Different models' vector spaces are unrelated. Cosine
  similarity between a BERT vector and a GPT vector is meaningless.
- **Ignoring tokenizer cost asymmetry.** Multilingual apps have a real, per-language cost
  difference — measure it.

## Exercises

**Understand**
1. Why does subword tokenization eliminate the out-of-vocabulary problem?
2. Explain, in tokenization terms, why LLMs fail to count letters.
3. Static vs contextual embeddings — where does each live in a transformer?
4. Why does word2vec need negative samples?

**Build**
1. Tokenize the same paragraph with BERT, GPT-2, GPT-4 (`tiktoken`), and a multilingual model.
   Print every token. Note the differences — this exercise pays for itself.
2. Measure the token cost of the same sentence in English, Hindi, Thai, and Arabic. Compute the
   price ratio. **This is a real production concern.**
3. Extract GPT-2's embedding matrix and find each of these words' nearest neighbours by cosine
   similarity: "king", "bank", "python". Note that "bank" has only one vector.
4. Train word2vec on a corpus of your own (Alammar's playlist example, or any co-occurrence data)
   and inspect what the embeddings learned.
5. Compare mean-pooled GPT-2 vectors against `sentence-transformers` embeddings on a small
   semantic-similarity task. Quantify how much worse the naive approach is.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: How does Byte Pair Encoding build its vocabulary?
A: Start from individual characters/bytes, repeatedly count adjacent pairs in the corpus and merge the most frequent pair into a new token, until reaching the target vocabulary size. Frequent words become single tokens; rare words stay split.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why can't an LLM count the letters in a word?
A: It never sees letters. "strawberry" arrives as something like `["straw", "berry"]` — two integers. Counting characters requires introspecting a representation the model does not have.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does the same sentence cost more tokens in Thai than in English?
A: The tokenizer's vocabulary was learned from a mostly-English corpus, so other languages fragment into many more subword pieces. The cost difference can be 3–5×, which is a real production concern for multilingual apps.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the difference between the embedding matrix and contextual embeddings?
A: The embedding matrix is a static lookup table (vocab_size × dim) — one context-free vector per token ID. Contextual embeddings are what each position holds *after* passing through the transformer's layers, shaped by surrounding tokens. Both exist in the same model.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does word2vec need negative sampling?
A: Without negatives the classifier trivially answers "related" to everything and all vectors collapse to one point. Contrast between real neighbours and random words is what creates the geometric structure.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why shouldn't you mean-pool a generative LLM's token vectors for semantic search?
A: Those representations were optimized for next-token prediction, not for cosine similarity between whole texts. Embedding models are trained with a contrastive objective specifically to make that similarity meaningful.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why do `"the"` and `" the"` behave differently in a prompt?
A: In most BPE tokenizers the leading space is part of the token, so they are two distinct IDs. This is why trailing whitespace in a prompt can measurably change model output.
<!-- /card -->
