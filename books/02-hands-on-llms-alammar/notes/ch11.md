# A11 — Fine-Tuning Representation Models for Classification

> **Thesis:** When frozen embeddings plus a linear head are not enough, the next step is not a
> bigger model — it is adapting the encoder itself, and there are techniques that do it with
> remarkably few labels.

## Why this chapter matters

It closes the loop on [A4](ch04.md)'s ladder. And it contains **SetFit**, which is genuinely one
of the most useful practical techniques in the book: competitive classification from **8 examples
per class**. If you have ever been blocked by "we don't have labeled data," this is the answer.

## Core concepts

### Full fine-tuning of an encoder

Add a classification head to BERT and train **all** parameters on your labeled data.

This is [G11](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch11.md)'s transfer learning, unchanged: the
pretrained encoder already understands language; you are teaching it your task.

Two variants:

- **Freeze the encoder, train the head only.** Fast, needs less data, but limited — it is
  equivalent to [A4](ch04.md)'s embeddings+classifier.
- **Train everything.** Better quality, needs more data, risks catastrophic forgetting.

Practical settings: small learning rate (2e-5 to 5e-5 — far smaller than training from scratch),
2–4 epochs (more overfits fast on small sets), warmup, and early stopping on validation.

**Freezing selectively.** Lower layers encode general language; upper layers are task-specific.
Freezing the bottom *n* layers is a cheap regularizer that often improves small-data results —
the same "lower layers are generic" logic as [G14](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch14.md).

### Domain adaptation via continued pretraining

If your domain is far from the pretraining corpus — clinical notes, legal contracts, internal
jargon — run **masked language modeling** on your *unlabeled* domain text before fine-tuning on
labels.

You almost always have far more unlabeled domain text than labeled examples, and MLM needs no
labels ([G17](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch17.md) — it is a denoising objective). This is
often the highest-value step available when labels are scarce and domain text is not.

### SetFit — the few-shot technique worth memorizing

Sentence Transformer Fine-Tuning. Three steps:

1. **Generate pairs from your few labels.** Any two examples with the *same* label form a
   positive pair; different labels form a negative pair. **With 8 examples per class over 2
   classes you get hundreds of pairs from 16 labels** — the combinatorics do the work.
2. **Fine-tune the sentence embedder contrastively** on those pairs ([A10](ch10.md)), so the
   space reorganizes around *your* notion of similarity.
3. **Train a logistic regression head** on the resulting embeddings.

Results are competitive with full fine-tuning on hundreds of examples, from ~8 per class. No
prompts, no generative model, tiny and fast at inference, and it gives you probabilities and a
tunable threshold.

> The insight — **turn a classification problem into a similarity problem to multiply your
> labels** — is worth carrying beyond this specific tool.

### Masked language modeling as continued pretraining

Mask ~15% of tokens, predict them. Mechanically the denoising autoencoder from
[G17](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch17.md), and the reason BERT works at all.

Two flavors:
- **Token masking** — mask individual tokens.
- **Whole-word masking** — mask all subword pieces of a word together. Harder and generally
  better, because it prevents the model from reconstructing a word from its own fragments.

### The choice, summarized

| Situation | Do this |
|---|---|
| Lots of labels (10k+) | Full fine-tuning |
| Moderate labels (1k–10k) | Fine-tune with frozen lower layers |
| **Few labels (10–100)** | **SetFit** |
| No labels, lots of domain text | Continued MLM pretraining, then any of the above |
| No labels at all | Zero-shot ([A4](ch04.md)) or a generative model |
| Labels changing constantly | Generative model with prompts |

## Key code

```python
# SetFit: competitive classification from ~8 examples per class
from setfit import SetFitModel, SetFitTrainer
from datasets import Dataset

model = SetFitModel.from_pretrained("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
trainer = SetFitTrainer(
    model=model,
    train_dataset=Dataset.from_dict({"text": texts, "label": labels}),  # 16 rows is fine
    num_iterations=20,        # contrastive pairs generated per example
    num_epochs=1,
)
trainer.train()
preds = model(["a new example to classify"])

# Standard encoder fine-tuning — note the very small learning rate
from transformers import AutoModelForSequenceClassification, TrainingArguments, Trainer

model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased", num_labels=3)
for layer in model.bert.encoder.layer[:6]:       # freeze the generic lower half
    for p in layer.parameters():
        p.requires_grad = False

args = TrainingArguments(learning_rate=2e-5, num_train_epochs=3,
                         per_device_train_batch_size=16, warmup_ratio=0.1,
                         eval_strategy="epoch", load_best_model_at_end=True)
```

## Gotchas

- **Learning rate too high.** 1e-3 will destroy pretrained weights in a few steps. Use 2e-5–5e-5.
- **Too many epochs.** Encoders overfit small datasets within 3–4 epochs. Early-stop on validation.
- **Skipping the frozen-embeddings baseline.** You need [A4](ch04.md)'s number to know whether
  fine-tuning earned its complexity.
- **Fine-tuning when you have 20 labels.** Use SetFit.
- **Ignoring class imbalance.** Weight the loss or resample, as in
  [G3](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch03.md).
- **Continued pretraining on too little text.** MLM on a few hundred documents is not worth the
  time; it wants tens of thousands.
- **Forgetting `max_length`.** Silent truncation of long documents.
- **Not saving the tokenizer with the model.** They must stay together.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: What learning rate should you use to fine-tune a pretrained encoder, and why?
A: 2e-5 to 5e-5 — roughly two orders of magnitude below training from scratch. The pretrained weights are already good; large updates destroy them (catastrophic forgetting).
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: How does SetFit work?
A: (1) Generate contrastive pairs from few labels — same-label pairs are positives, different-label pairs are negatives, so 16 examples yield hundreds of pairs. (2) Fine-tune a sentence embedder on those pairs. (3) Train a logistic-regression head on the resulting embeddings.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is SetFit's transferable insight?
A: Turning a classification problem into a similarity problem multiplies your labeled data combinatorially, because pairs are quadratic in examples.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: When should you do continued MLM pretraining?
A: When your domain is far from the pretraining corpus (clinical, legal, internal jargon) and you have far more unlabeled domain text than labels. MLM needs no labels, so it converts the resource you have into the improvement you need.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does freezing lower layers help on small datasets?
A: Lower layers encode general language features that transfer; upper layers are task-specific. Freezing the bottom reduces trainable parameters, which regularizes and prevents overfitting when data is scarce.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Token masking vs whole-word masking in MLM?
A: Token masking hides individual subword tokens; whole-word masking hides all subword pieces of a word together. Whole-word is harder and generally better, because it stops the model reconstructing a word from its own surviving fragments.
<!-- /card -->
