# A9 — Multimodal Large Language Models

> **Thesis:** If you can project images into the same vector space as text, an image becomes just
> another sequence of tokens — and the language model needs almost no modification.

## Why this chapter matters

Marked **Context** in the roadmap, but the central trick is elegant and worth understanding
because it generalizes: **any modality you can embed into the model's representation space becomes
usable by the model.** Audio, video, and structured data all follow the same recipe.

CLIP's contrastive training is also the third appearance of the pattern from
[A2](ch02.md) — positives, negatives, learn a space where similar things are close.

## Core concepts

### Vision Transformers

Transformers process sequences of vectors. Images are grids of pixels. The bridge is embarrassingly
simple:

1. Cut the image into fixed patches (16×16 pixels).
2. Flatten each patch and linearly project it to `d_model`.
3. Add positional embeddings.
4. Feed the sequence to a standard transformer.

**A patch is a token.** That is the whole idea. A 224×224 image at 16×16 patches gives 196
tokens — a short sequence by language standards.

ViTs lack the CNN's inductive biases of locality and translation invariance
([G14](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch14.md)), so they need much more data. Given enough,
they surpass CNNs — which is the general lesson about inductive bias: constraints help when data
is scarce and hurt when it is abundant.

### CLIP — the alignment trick

Two encoders, one image and one text, trained so that **matching pairs are close and
non-matching pairs are far** in a shared space.

The training objective on a batch of *N* (image, caption) pairs:
- Compute all *N × N* similarities.
- The *N* diagonal entries are positives; the *N² − N* off-diagonal are negatives.
- Symmetric cross-entropy across rows and columns.

The batch supplies its own negatives, which is why CLIP wants **very large batches** — more
negatives means a harder, more informative task.

What this buys:
- **Zero-shot classification.** Embed the candidate labels as text ("a photo of a dog"), embed
  the image, take the nearest. Exactly the zero-shot trick from [A4](ch04.md), across modalities.
- **Cross-modal retrieval.** Search images with text and vice versa.
- **A conditioning signal** for generative image models.

> Same recipe as word2vec ([A2](ch02.md)) and sentence embeddings ([A10](ch10.md)): define
> positives, sample negatives, learn a space. Three chapters, one idea. Once you see it, you can
> read most representation-learning papers.

### Multimodal LLMs

To give a language model eyes:

1. Take a pretrained vision encoder (often CLIP's).
2. Take a pretrained LLM.
3. Train a **projection layer** mapping image embeddings into the LLM's token embedding space.
4. Feed the projected image vectors as if they were token embeddings.

**Usually only the projector is trained**, at least initially — both large models stay frozen.
That is what makes multimodal training tractable: you are training an adapter, not a model. It is
the same economy as LoRA ([H7](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch07.md)) — a small trainable
bridge between large frozen components.

BLIP-2's Q-Former is a more elaborate version: a small transformer that queries the image
representation and produces a fixed number of vectors for the LLM, which also controls how many
tokens an image costs.

### Practical notes

- **Images consume a lot of context.** A high-resolution image can cost 1,000+ tokens. Multiple
  images blow through the window and the bill quickly.
- **Resolution is a real tradeoff.** Fine detail (small text, charts) needs high resolution, which
  costs tokens. Many models tile large images, multiplying the cost.
- **OCR-ish tasks are unreliable.** Reading dense text in an image is a known weak point;
  dedicated OCR into text is often better and cheaper.
- **Multimodal RAG** — embed images and text into a shared space and retrieve across both. See
  [H6](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch06.md).

import torch

## Gotchas

- **Comparing embeddings across models.** CLIP image vectors and a sentence-transformer's text
  vectors live in unrelated spaces. Only vectors from the *same* CLIP model are comparable.
- **Forgetting to normalize** before cosine similarity.
- **Bare label words in zero-shot.** "a photo of a {label}" substantially outperforms "{label}" —
  it matches the caption distribution CLIP was trained on. Prompt engineering, for images.
- **Underestimating image token cost.** Check the provider's accounting; it is easy to 10× your
  bill.
- **Relying on a VLM for dense OCR.** Use a real OCR pipeline and pass text.
- **Assuming spatial precision.** VLMs are weak at exact positions, counting objects, and reading
  precise values off charts.

## Exercises

**Understand**
1. How does a Vision Transformer turn an image into a sequence?
2. Where do CLIP's negative examples come from, and why does batch size matter so much?
3. Why is only the projection layer trained in many multimodal LLMs?
4. Why does "a photo of a cat" beat "cat" for zero-shot classification?

**Build**
1. Zero-shot image classification with CLIP on your own photos. Try five prompt templates for the
   same labels and measure the spread.
2. Build a text-to-image search over a personal photo folder. It is genuinely useful and takes
   about 30 lines.
3. Ask a multimodal model to read a dense chart, then transcribe the same chart yourself. Catalog
   the errors — this calibrates how much to trust VLMs on precise data.
4. Measure the token cost of one image at several resolutions with your provider, and compute the
   break-even against OCR-then-text.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: How does a Vision Transformer feed an image to a transformer?
A: Cut the image into fixed patches (e.g. 16×16 pixels), flatten and linearly project each patch to d_model, add positional embeddings, and treat the result as a token sequence. A patch is a token.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: How is CLIP trained?
A: Two encoders (image and text) trained contrastively on (image, caption) pairs. Within a batch of N pairs, the N diagonal similarities are positives and the N²−N off-diagonal ones are negatives, with symmetric cross-entropy. Large batches matter because the batch supplies the negatives.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: How do you give a language model vision without retraining it?
A: Take a pretrained vision encoder and a pretrained LLM, freeze both, and train only a projection layer that maps image embeddings into the LLM's token embedding space. Projected image vectors are then fed as if they were token embeddings.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does "a photo of a cat" beat "cat" for CLIP zero-shot classification?
A: CLIP was trained on image–caption pairs, so full caption-like sentences match its training distribution. Bare label words are off-distribution. It is prompt engineering applied to images.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the recurring contrastive-learning recipe across word2vec, CLIP, and sentence embeddings?
A: Define positive pairs, sample or mine negatives, and train so positives are close and negatives far in a shared space. The differences are only in what counts as a pair and where the negatives come from.
<!-- /card -->
