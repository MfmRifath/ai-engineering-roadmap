# H7 — Finetuning

> **Thesis:** The default answer is *don't* — and knowing precisely why is more valuable than
> knowing how.

## Why this chapter matters

Alammar ([A12](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch12.md)) taught you the mechanics. This
chapter teaches the **decision**, and it is the more valuable half. Most fine-tuning projects
should not have happened; they were expensive ways to solve a problem prompting or RAG would have
solved in an afternoon.

The memory math section is also the most practically useful arithmetic in the five books.

## Core concepts

### When to fine-tune, and when not to

Huyen's ladder — **exhaust each rung before climbing**:

1. **Better prompting** — cheapest, fastest to iterate, no infrastructure.
2. **Better context (RAG)** — for knowledge problems. Updatable, citable, auditable.
3. **Fine-tuning** — for behaviour, format, style, and domain-specific patterns.

**Reasons to fine-tune:**
- **Consistent format or style** you cannot get reliably from prompts
- **Domain-specific behaviour** — jargon, conventions, reasoning patterns
- **Latency and cost** — a fine-tuned small model can replace a prompted large one, which is
  often the strongest business case
- **Distilling** a large model's behaviour into a small one
- **Shortening prompts** — behaviour baked into weights costs no tokens per request

**Reasons NOT to fine-tune** — the more important list:
- **To add knowledge.** Fine-tuning is a bad knowledge store: expensive to update, un-citable,
  and it does not reliably override what the model already believes. **Use RAG.**
- **You lack quality data.** The dataset is the work ([H8](ch08.md)), and a bad one makes the
  model worse.
- **The base model has not been given a fair chance.** Prompt properly first.
- **You need to track a moving frontier.** A fine-tune pins you to a base model; the next
  generation may beat your tuned version out of the box.
- **You have no evaluation set.** You cannot detect catastrophic forgetting, and you will not know
  whether you improved anything.

### RAG or fine-tuning? The clean split

| Problem | Use |
|---|---|
| The model doesn't **know** something | **RAG** |
| The model doesn't **behave** how you want | **Fine-tuning** |
| Both | RAG first, then fine-tune the behaviour |

Memorize this. It resolves the argument almost every time it comes up.

### Memory math — the arithmetic to internalize

**Inference** memory ≈ parameters × bytes-per-parameter, plus KV cache and activations:

| Precision | Bytes/param | 7B model |
|---|---|---|
| fp32 | 4 | 28 GB |
| fp16/bf16 | 2 | **14 GB** |
| int8 | 1 | 7 GB |
| int4 | 0.5 | **3.5 GB** |

**Training** is far worse, and the breakdown is why:

| Component | Cost | 7B in fp16 |
|---|---|---|
| Weights | 2 bytes/param | 14 GB |
| Gradients | 2 bytes/param | 14 GB |
| Adam states (m, v in fp32) | 8 bytes/param | **56 GB** |
| Activations | batch × seq × layers | Varies, often large |
| **Total** | | **~90 GB+** |

**Adam's optimizer states are the villain** — two fp32 moments per parameter, four times the
weight memory. That single fact is why PEFT exists and why it works: eliminate trainable
parameters and the 56 GB disappears with them.

**Activation memory** scales with batch size, sequence length, and depth, because backprop caches
every layer's forward activations ([G10](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch10.md)). **Gradient
checkpointing** trades compute for memory here — recompute activations during the backward pass
instead of storing them, typically ~30% slower for a large memory saving.

### Quantization

Store weights in fewer bits with a scale (and zero-point) per group
([G19](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch19.md)).

- **Post-training quantization** — quantize a trained model. Cheap, small quality loss.
- **Quantization-aware training** — simulate quantization during training so the model adapts.
  Better quality, more work.
- **Inference vs training quantization** — inference quantization is routine and low-risk;
  quantizing gradients is harder.

Formats worth knowing: **NF4** (normal-float, matched to weights' roughly-Gaussian distribution),
**GPTQ**, **AWQ**, **GGUF** (the llama.cpp family). Rule of thumb: **int8 is nearly free; int4
costs a little; below 4-bit degrades noticeably.** Always measure on your own task rather than
trusting a published table.

### PEFT

**LoRA** — freeze W, learn a low-rank update BA
([A12](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch12.md)). Trainable parameters drop to ~0.1–1%, so
gradients and optimizer states drop with them.

**QLoRA** — LoRA over a 4-bit quantized base. 7B fine-tunable on 16 GB.

Other approaches — adapters, prefix tuning, prompt tuning — exist; LoRA won on the quality/
simplicity tradeoff.

**Merging and multi-task.** Multiple LoRAs can be merged, or served simultaneously against one
base model, which is a strong serving story: one 14 GB base plus a dozen 10 MB adapters rather
than a dozen fine-tuned models.

**Model merging** — combining weights of separately-trained models — is worth knowing as a cheap
alternative to multi-task training.

### Tactics

- **Start small.** Fewer examples, smaller model, fewer epochs. Establish that the pipeline works
  before scaling it.
- **1–3 epochs.** More overfits fast on small datasets.
- **Low learning rate** (1e-5 to 1e-4 for LoRA; lower for full).
- **Evaluate for catastrophic forgetting**, not just the target task. A model that got better at
  your task and worse at everything else is usually a regression.
- **Keep the base model available** for comparison and rollback.

## Key code

```python
def training_memory_gb(n_params_b: float, bytes_per_param: int = 2,
                       optimizer: str = "adamw", lora_frac: float = 1.0) -> dict:
    """Back-of-envelope training memory. Run this BEFORE renting a GPU."""
    p = n_params_b * 1e9
    trainable = p * lora_frac
    weights = p * bytes_per_param / 1e9
    grads = trainable * bytes_per_param / 1e9
    opt = trainable * (8 if optimizer == "adamw" else 0) / 1e9   # fp32 m and v
    return {"weights": weights, "gradients": grads, "optimizer": opt,
            "total_excl_activations": weights + grads + opt}

print(training_memory_gb(7))                     # full: ~84 GB
print(training_memory_gb(7, lora_frac=0.005))    # LoRA: ~14.4 GB
print(training_memory_gb(7, bytes_per_param=0.5, lora_frac=0.005))  # QLoRA: ~3.9 GB
```

That function is worth keeping. It turns "will this fit?" from a guess into arithmetic.

## Gotchas

- **Fine-tuning to add knowledge.** The single most common expensive mistake. Use RAG.
- **No baseline.** Measure the prompted base model on your eval set first, or you cannot claim an
  improvement.
- **No catastrophic-forgetting check.** Evaluate general capability too, not only the target task.
- **Too many epochs.** 1–3. Overfitting on small sets is fast and quiet.
- **Learning rate too high.** Destroys pretrained knowledge.
- **Forgetting activation memory.** The parameter arithmetic is only part of the total.
- **Sub-4-bit quantization without measurement.** Degradation becomes real below 4-bit.
- **Merging a LoRA into a quantized base.** Merge into fp16, or you compound quantization error.
- **Fine-tuning a model that will be superseded next quarter.** Consider the frontier's pace.
- **Not keeping the base model.** You need it for comparison and rollback.

## Exercises

**Understand**
1. State the RAG-vs-fine-tuning rule in one sentence.
2. Why do Adam's optimizer states dominate training memory?
3. How does LoRA eliminate that cost?
4. What does gradient checkpointing trade, and by how much?
5. Give four reasons *not* to fine-tune.

**Build**
1. Implement the memory calculator above. Compute requirements for 1B/7B/70B under full, LoRA,
   and QLoRA. **Check the numbers against a real run** — the gap is activation memory.
2. Take a task you might fine-tune for. Try prompting properly, then RAG, and measure both.
   Only then fine-tune, and compare against those baselines. Most of the time you will stop early
   — that is the exercise working.
3. QLoRA fine-tune a small model and evaluate on **both** the target task and a general benchmark.
   Quantify catastrophic forgetting.
4. Measure quality at fp16, int8, int4, and int3 on your own task. Find where it breaks.
5. Train two LoRAs for different tasks and serve both against one base model.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: RAG or fine-tuning — what is the rule?
A: If the model doesn't *know* something, use RAG. If the model doesn't *behave* how you want, fine-tune. If both, do RAG first and fine-tune the behaviour.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does full fine-tuning of a 7B model need ~90 GB?
A: 14 GB weights (fp16) + 14 GB gradients + **56 GB Adam optimizer states** (two fp32 moments per parameter) + activations. The optimizer states are four times the weight memory — they are the reason PEFT exists.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Give four reasons NOT to fine-tune.
A: To add knowledge (use RAG — it's updatable and citable); you lack quality data; you haven't properly tried prompting; you need to track a moving frontier (a fine-tune pins you to a base model); or you have no evaluation set to detect regressions.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is gradient checkpointing and what does it cost?
A: Instead of caching every layer's forward activations for the backward pass, recompute them on demand. It trades roughly 30% extra compute for a large reduction in activation memory.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the practical quantization quality curve?
A: int8 is nearly free; int4 costs a little; below 4 bits degradation becomes noticeable. Always measure on your own task rather than trusting a published table.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the strongest business case for fine-tuning?
A: Replacing a prompted large model with a fine-tuned small one — cutting latency and per-request cost simultaneously — and shortening prompts, since behaviour baked into weights costs no tokens per call.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is catastrophic forgetting, and how do you detect it?
A: Fine-tuning degrades capabilities outside the target task. Detect it by evaluating on a general benchmark as well as your task — a model that improved on one and regressed everywhere else is usually a net loss.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What serving advantage do LoRA adapters give?
A: One base model (say 14 GB) can serve many tasks by swapping or simultaneously serving small adapters (~10 MB each), instead of hosting a dozen separately fine-tuned full models.
