# H1 — Introduction to Building AI Applications with Foundation Models

> **Thesis:** AI engineering is a distinct discipline from ML engineering — it starts with a
> model that already exists, so the work moves from *training* to *adapting, evaluating, and
> operating*.

## Why this chapter matters

Marked **Skimmable**, but two sections earn real attention: the **AI Engineering vs ML
Engineering** comparison, which names what actually changed, and the **planning** section, which
is the most commonly skipped step in real projects and the most expensive to skip.

## Core concepts

### How the discipline shifted

| | ML Engineering | AI Engineering |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | No model | A powerful model exists |
| Main effort | Train a model | Adapt and evaluate one |
| Data need | Large labeled datasets | Small, high-quality sets |
| Iteration | Days–weeks (retraining) | Minutes (prompting) |
| Hard part | Getting it to work at all | **Knowing whether it works** |
| Compute | Training-dominated | Inference-dominated |

That last-but-one row is Huyen's thesis for the whole book. When a demo takes an afternoon, the
bottleneck moves entirely to evaluation — and most teams never make that shift, which is why so
many LLM projects stall between "impressive demo" and "shipped."

It also inverts the cost model. In classical ML, training dominated. With foundation models you
pay per request, forever, which makes [H9](ch09.md) a business concern rather than an
optimization exercise.

### The three-layer stack

1. **Application development** — prompts, context, interface, evaluation. Where most people work.
2. **Model development** — fine-tuning, dataset engineering, inference optimization.
3. **Infrastructure** — serving, monitoring, orchestration, compute.

Useful mainly as a locator: when something is wrong, which layer owns it? A hallucination is
usually layer 1 (context), sometimes layer 2 (the model), rarely layer 3.

### Use case categories

Coding, image/video, writing, education, conversational bots, information aggregation, data
organization, workflow automation.

The pattern worth extracting: foundation models excel where **output is text-shaped, tolerance for
imperfection is nonzero, and a human is in the loop or verification is cheap.** They struggle
where output must be exactly right, unverifiable, and unsupervised.

That heuristic will save you from more bad projects than any technical knowledge.

### Planning — the part everyone skips

**Use case evaluation.** Ask, in order:
- What does the AI do that a simpler system cannot?
- What is the cost of being wrong, and who bears it?
- Is this defensible, or is it a feature the model provider will ship next quarter?

**Setting expectations.** Define what "good enough" means *numerically* **before** you build.
Huyen is blunt: without a target, you will ship on vibes and argue about quality forever. This is
the discipline that [H4](ch04.md) makes concrete.

**Milestone planning.** A demo is 20% of the work. The gap between demo and production is where
projects die, and it is almost entirely evaluation, edge cases, latency, and cost.

**Maintenance.** Models get deprecated. Providers change behaviour under you without changing the
version string. Prompts that worked stop working. Plan for it — [H10](ch10.md) is the
architecture that makes it survivable.

### AI engineering vs full-stack engineering

Huyen's observation is sharp: AI engineering pulls product engineers toward AI rather than pulling
ML researchers toward products, because the model is now an API. The scarce skill is not modeling
— it is **evaluation and systems judgment.**

## Key code

None. This chapter is framing.

The one artifact worth producing, before writing any code for a new project:

```markdown
## Use case: <name>
Baseline today:            <what happens without AI>
Success metric:            <number, measured how>
Acceptable failure rate:   <number> and who absorbs it
Cost ceiling:              <$/request or $/month>
Latency ceiling:           <p50 / p95 ms>
Kill criteria:             <what would make us stop>
```

If you cannot fill it in, you are not ready to build. Keep it in the repo next to the code.

## Gotchas

- **Building before defining "good enough."** You will never be able to say whether you succeeded.
- **Ignoring the inference cost model.** Per-request costs scale with usage in a way training
  costs do not. Model it early.
- **Assuming demo quality predicts production quality.** Demos are chosen examples. Production is
  the tail.
- **Building what the provider will ship next quarter.** Ask what is defensible.
- **No deprecation plan.** Models are retired and silently updated. Abstract the provider.
- **Using an LLM where a regex or a classifier would do.** Cheaper, faster, testable, deterministic.

## Exercises

**Understand**
1. Name three concrete ways AI engineering differs from ML engineering.
2. Why does evaluation become the bottleneck when demos are easy?
3. What properties make a use case well suited to foundation models?

**Build**
1. Fill in the planning template above for a project you actually want to build. Be specific
   about the numbers.
2. For that project, write down the **non-AI baseline** — what a rules-based or classical-ML
   version would achieve. You need this to know whether the AI is earning its cost.
3. Estimate cost per request and monthly cost at 1k, 100k, and 10M requests. The shape of that
   curve changes product decisions.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the central difference between ML engineering and AI engineering?
A: ML engineering starts with no model and the hard part is making one work. AI engineering starts with a powerful existing model, so the hard part becomes *knowing whether it works* — evaluation — and the cost model shifts from training-dominated to inference-dominated.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does evaluation become the bottleneck in AI engineering?
A: Because building a demo takes hours instead of weeks. When the build step collapses, everything downstream — measuring quality, catching regressions, handling the tail — becomes the constraint. Most stalled LLM projects are stuck exactly there.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What properties make a use case well suited to foundation models?
A: Text-shaped output, nonzero tolerance for imperfection, and either a human in the loop or cheap verification. They struggle where output must be exactly right, is unverifiable, and runs unsupervised.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What should you define before building an AI feature?
A: The numeric success metric and how it is measured, the acceptable failure rate and who absorbs it, cost and latency ceilings, the non-AI baseline, and kill criteria. Without these you ship on vibes.
<!-- /card -->
