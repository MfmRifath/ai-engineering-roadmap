# H8 — Dataset Engineering

> **Thesis:** The dataset is the model. Quality beats quantity, coverage beats both, and this is
> where the actual work of fine-tuning lives.

## Why this chapter matters

Everyone wants to talk about architectures and nobody wants to talk about data, which is precisely
why data is where the differentiation is. [H7](ch07.md) told you when to fine-tune; this chapter
is what you spend the time on if you do.

It also connects back to [G2](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch02.md) — the discipline is the
same, only the scale and the failure modes changed.

## Core concepts

### The three axes of data curation

**Quality.** Correct, well-formatted, consistent, unambiguous. Huyen's evidence — and the LIMA
result — is that **a thousand high-quality examples beat ten thousand mediocre ones**, sometimes
by a lot. Bad examples do not merely fail to help; they actively teach the wrong thing.

**Coverage.** Does the data span the distribution you will actually see? Edge cases, input
lengths, user segments, languages, tones. This is
[G1](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch01.md)'s sampling bias, and it is the failure that hides
best — your model looks great on your eval set because both were drawn from the same narrow slice.

**Quantity.** Matters least of the three, and depends on the method:

| Task | Rough scale |
|---|---|
| Instruction tuning (behaviour) | ~1,000 good examples |
| Classification fine-tune | 100s–1,000s per class |
| Domain adaptation (continued pretraining) | Millions of tokens |
| Preference tuning | 1,000s of comparisons |

### Acquisition and annotation

Sources: existing product data (best — it is your real distribution), public datasets (check
licences), purchased, synthetic.

**Annotation is where quality is made or lost.** The essentials:
- **A clear guideline with worked examples.** The same artifact as
  [H4](ch04.md)'s evaluation guideline, and for the same reason.
- **Measure inter-annotator agreement.** If humans disagree, the task is under-specified and no
  model will do better than the disagreement floor.
- **Multiple annotators on a sample**, with adjudication.
- **Active learning** — annotate the examples the model is least confident about, which is
  [G9](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch09.md)'s cluster-representative argument.

### Synthetic data

**Why:** cheap, scalable, controllable, and privacy-preserving. Often the only way to get coverage
of rare cases.

**Traditional techniques** — templates, back-translation, paraphrase, perturbation.

**AI-powered** — have a model generate the data. Huyen's practical patterns:
- **Self-instruct** — seed with a few human examples, have the model generate more.
- **Evol-instruct** — iteratively make instructions harder or more specific.
- **Generate the input, not the output.** Underrated: given documents, generate plausible
  *queries* for them. Verifying "is this a reasonable question for this document?" is far easier
  than verifying a generated answer. This is how you bootstrap retrieval eval sets.

**Model distillation** — train a small model on a large one's outputs. Very effective, and it is
the mechanism behind most small models that punch above their weight. **Check the licence** —
many providers' terms prohibit training competing models on their outputs.

**The risks, which are real:**
- **Quality ceiling** — synthetic data inherits the generator's errors and blind spots.
- **Diversity collapse** — models generate a narrower distribution than reality. Repeated
  generations cluster.
- **Model collapse** — training on synthetic data recursively degrades the distribution over
  generations.
- **Contamination** — synthetic data derived from benchmarks poisons your evaluation.

Mitigations: mix synthetic with real data, verify programmatically where possible, and
**never generate your evaluation set with the model you are evaluating**.

### Data processing

**Inspect the data.** Huyen is emphatic and correct: *look at your data*. Read a hundred random
examples. You will find encoding errors, truncation, template leakage, and duplicates that no
summary statistic would surface. This is the highest value-per-hour activity in the chapter.

**Deduplicate.** Exact duplicates, near-duplicates (MinHash/LSH), and semantic duplicates
(embedding similarity). Duplicates cause memorization, waste compute, and — worst — leak between
train and test, inflating your metrics invisibly.

**Clean and filter** — remove PII, low quality, toxic content, wrong language, and anything
outside your intended distribution.

**Format** — apply the model's chat template consistently, mask the loss on prompt tokens
([R7](../../03-build-llm-from-scratch-raschka/notes/ch07.md)), and check length distributions
against the context limit.

## Key code

```python
# Near-duplicate detection: the leak that inflates your metrics silently
from datasketch import MinHash, MinHashLSH

def dedupe(texts, threshold=0.8, num_perm=128):
    lsh = MinHashLSH(threshold=threshold, num_perm=num_perm)
    keep = []
    for i, text in enumerate(texts):
        m = MinHash(num_perm=num_perm)
        for shingle in {text[j:j+5] for j in range(len(text) - 4)}:
            m.update(shingle.encode())
        if not lsh.query(m):
            lsh.insert(str(i), m)
            keep.append(i)
    return keep
```

## Gotchas

- **Not looking at the data.** The single most common and most costly omission. Read 100 examples.
- **Duplicates between train and eval.** Inflates metrics invisibly. Dedupe *across* splits.
- **Optimizing quantity over quality.** A thousand good examples beat ten thousand mediocre ones.
- **Coverage measured only against your eval set.** If both came from the same narrow slice, both
  look fine and production does not.
- **Synthetic evaluation data from the model under test.** Circular; you are measuring agreement
  with itself.
- **Diversity collapse.** Generated data clusters more tightly than reality. Mix in real data.
- **Ignoring distillation licences.** Many providers prohibit training competitors on outputs.
- **Inconsistent formatting.** Teaches inconsistency ([R7](../../03-build-llm-from-scratch-raschka/notes/ch07.md)).
- **Not measuring inter-annotator agreement.** If humans can't agree, the spec is broken and the
  model cannot beat that floor.
- **PII left in training data.** It can be regurgitated. Filter before training, not after an
  incident.

## Exercises

**Understand**
1. Rank quality, coverage, and quantity, and justify the ordering.
2. Why does generating *inputs* work better than generating outputs?
3. What is model collapse, and how do you avoid it?
4. Why does inter-annotator agreement bound model performance?

**Build**
1. **Read 100 random examples from any dataset you plan to use.** Write down every problem you
   find. This exercise pays for itself immediately, every time.
2. Deduplicate a dataset at exact, near (MinHash), and semantic (embedding) levels. Report how
   many each stage removed — the near-duplicate count usually surprises people.
3. Check for train/eval leakage by looking for near-duplicates *across* splits.
4. Generate synthetic queries for your [p2](../../../projects/p2-rag-over-my-library/) corpus and
   use them as a retrieval eval set. You get ground truth for free.
5. Do a coverage analysis: cluster training data and production traffic in the same embedding
   space and find the clusters your training data misses.
6. Compare a model fine-tuned on 1,000 curated examples against one trained on 10,000 unfiltered
   ones.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: Rank quality, coverage, and quantity for a fine-tuning dataset.
A: Quality first — a thousand good examples beat ten thousand mediocre ones, and bad examples actively teach the wrong thing. Coverage second — it is the failure that hides best. Quantity last, and it depends heavily on the method.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why is generating synthetic *inputs* easier than generating outputs?
A: Verification is easier. Checking "is this a reasonable question for this passage?" is far simpler than checking whether a generated answer is correct — and since you know which passage produced each question, you get retrieval ground truth for free.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is model collapse?
A: Recursive training on synthetic data progressively narrows and degrades the data distribution across generations, because each generation samples from — and slightly distorts — the last. Mitigate by mixing in real data and verifying programmatically.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why must you deduplicate *across* train and eval splits?
A: Near-duplicates spanning the split leak test data into training, inflating your metrics with no visible symptom. Exact matching is not enough — use MinHash/LSH for near-duplicates and embeddings for semantic ones.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does inter-annotator agreement matter?
A: It is a ceiling. If human annotators disagree about the correct label, the task is under-specified and no model can reliably exceed that disagreement floor. Low agreement means fix the guideline, not the model.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the highest value-per-hour activity in dataset work?
A: Reading a hundred random examples. Encoding errors, truncation, template leakage, and duplicates are all invisible to summary statistics and obvious on inspection.
<!-- /card -->
