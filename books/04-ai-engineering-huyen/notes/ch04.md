# H4 — Evaluate AI Systems

> **Thesis:** Evaluating a *system* is not evaluating a model. You evaluate each component, against
> a written guideline, on data that reflects your actual traffic — and the guideline is the part
> that takes the longest and matters most.

## Why this chapter matters

[H3](ch03.md) gave you the methods. This chapter tells you how to assemble them into something a
team can operate. It also contains the **model selection workflow** and the **build vs buy**
framework, which are the two decisions you will make repeatedly.

## Core concepts

### Evaluation criteria — pick them before you build

**Domain-specific capability** — can it do the task at all? Coding, medical reasoning, your
domain's jargon. Often measured by benchmarks, with all the contamination caveats.

**Generation capability** — is the output any good? Fluency, coherence, faithfulness to source,
and **factual consistency** (does it contradict the provided context?).

**Instruction-following** — does it respect the format, constraints, and role you gave it?
Independently measurable and frequently the actual failure: the model knows the answer and
ignores your JSON schema.

**Cost and latency** — first-class criteria, not afterthoughts:

| Metric | Means | Driven by |
|---|---|---|
| **TTFT** (time to first token) | Responsiveness | Prompt length, prefill compute |
| **TPOT** (time per output token) | Streaming speed | Memory bandwidth, model size |
| **Total latency** | TTFT + TPOT × tokens | Both |
| **Throughput** | Requests/sec | Batching, hardware |
| **Cost/request** | Input + output tokens × price | Prompt design, output length |

Huyen's framing to keep: **quality, cost, and latency are a three-way trade** and you must state
your position explicitly. A team that has not decided will silently optimize for whichever one
someone complains about most recently.

### Model selection workflow

Huyen's four-stage funnel, and the ordering is the point — cheap filters first:

1. **Filter by hard constraints.** Licence, data residency, privacy, deployment mode, context
   length, modality. Non-negotiables eliminate most options for free.
2. **Screen with public benchmarks.** Cheap, coarse, contaminated. Use to shortlist 3–5, never to
   decide.
3. **Evaluate on your own data.** The decisive step. Your eval set, your criteria.
4. **Trial in production** with a small traffic slice, watching real metrics.

**Public benchmarks are for shortlisting, not deciding.** Contamination, task mismatch, and
saturation mean leaderboard rank frequently fails to predict performance on your task. Same
argument as MTEB in [A10](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch10.md).

### Build vs buy

| Dimension | Commercial API | Open weights, self-hosted |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | Usually frontier | Close, and closing |
| Time to first result | Minutes | Days |
| Cost at low volume | **Cheaper** | Fixed infra cost dominates |
| Cost at high volume | Expensive | **Cheaper** |
| Data privacy | Leaves your infrastructure | Stays put |
| Latency control | Provider's | Yours |
| Version stability | **Changes under you** | Pinned forever |
| Fine-tuning | Limited | Full |
| Ops burden | None | Real and ongoing |

Two rows deserve emphasis. **Version instability** is underrated: a provider can change model
behaviour without changing the version string, and your carefully tuned prompts silently degrade.
**Ops burden** is underestimated in the other direction — self-hosting is a real, permanent team
cost, not a one-time setup.

There is a crossover volume where self-hosting wins on cost. Compute it for your case rather than
arguing about it.

### Designing the evaluation pipeline

**Step 1 — Evaluate every component, not just the end.**

A RAG system is at minimum: query rewriting → retrieval → reranking → generation. If you measure
only the final answer, you cannot tell which stage failed. Retrieval has ground truth and is
cheaply measurable ([A8](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch08.md)); measure it separately and
you localize most failures immediately.

Also evaluate **per turn and per task** in multi-turn systems. A conversation can consist of
individually-fine turns that collectively fail the user's goal.

**Step 2 — Create an evaluation guideline.** Huyen calls this the most important and most skipped
step.

The guideline defines what "good" means, concretely:
- What the response must contain.
- What disqualifies it outright.
- Worked examples of good, borderline, and bad — with reasoning.
- How to score ambiguous cases.

Two reasons it matters more than it looks. First, **you cannot write it without deciding what you
actually want**, and that decision is usually where the disagreement was hiding. Second, it is the
prompt for your LLM judge and the instructions for your human annotators — the same artifact
serves both, which is what keeps them consistent.

**Step 3 — Define methods and data.**

- **Mix methods.** Functional correctness where possible, similarity where there are references,
  judges for the rest. Different failure modes, composed.
- **Slice your data.** Report per user segment, per query type, per language, per difficulty. An
  aggregate hides exactly the failures you need to see — the same argument as
  [G3](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch03.md)'s error analysis.
- **Size your eval set** for the effect you need to detect. To distinguish 80% from 82%, a
  50-example set is noise.
- **Annotate real production data**, not just synthetic examples. Your users write things you
  would never think to test.

### Evaluation as a living asset

Every production failure becomes a test case. Over time the eval set becomes your most valuable
artifact — it encodes everything you have learned about what breaks. It also decays: as the
system improves, the set stops discriminating, so it needs refreshing.

## Key code

```python
# Component-level evaluation: measure each stage, not just the end
def evaluate_rag_system(examples, pipeline):
    results = {"retrieval_recall": [], "answer_correct": [], "faithful": []}
    for ex in examples:
        chunks = pipeline.retrieve(ex.question)
        # Retrieval HAS ground truth — this is where most failures are, and it's cheap
        results["retrieval_recall"].append(
            len(set(c.id for c in chunks) & set(ex.relevant_ids)) / len(ex.relevant_ids)
        )
        answer = pipeline.generate(ex.question, chunks)
        results["answer_correct"].append(judge_correctness(answer, ex.reference))
        # Faithfulness is separate from correctness: is it supported by what we retrieved?
        results["faithful"].append(judge_faithfulness(answer, chunks))
    return {k: sum(v) / len(v) for k, v in results.items()}
```

```python
# Slice, always — the aggregate hides what you need to see
for segment in ["new_user", "power_user", "enterprise"]:
    subset = [e for e in examples if e.segment == segment]
    print(segment, evaluate_rag_system(subset, pipeline))
```

## Gotchas

- **End-to-end metrics only.** You will know it is broken and not where.
- **No evaluation guideline.** Your judge and your annotators drift apart, and you cannot
  adjudicate disputes.
- **Deciding on public benchmarks.** Shortlist with them; decide on your data.
- **An eval set too small for the difference you care about.** 50 examples cannot resolve 2%.
- **Only synthetic eval data.** Real users are stranger than you are.
- **Aggregate reporting.** Slice by segment, language, and difficulty.
- **Ignoring cost and latency until launch.** They are criteria, not constraints discovered late.
- **Assuming the API model is stable.** Pin versions where you can; monitor for drift where you
  cannot.
- **A static eval set.** Add every production failure. Refresh when it stops discriminating.

## Exercises

**Understand**
1. Why evaluate components separately rather than end to end?
2. Why is the evaluation guideline the most important artifact?
3. When does self-hosting beat an API, and on which axes?
4. Why are public benchmarks unsuitable as a decision criterion?

**Build**
1. **Write an evaluation guideline** for a task you care about: must-contain, disqualifiers, and
   three worked examples with reasoning. Then use it as both the judge prompt and your own
   rubric, and measure agreement between the two.
2. Build a component-level eval for a RAG pipeline. Report retrieval recall and answer quality
   separately, and find a case where retrieval succeeded but generation failed.
3. Run the four-stage model selection funnel for a real task across three models. Record every
   decision and what it cost.
4. Compute the crossover volume where self-hosting beats the API for your workload.
5. Slice one evaluation five ways and find a segment where performance is materially worse than
   the aggregate suggests.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: Why must you evaluate components separately rather than only end-to-end?
A: End-to-end scores tell you something is broken but not where. In a RAG system, retrieval has ground truth and is cheaply measurable, so measuring it separately localizes the majority of failures immediately.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is an evaluation guideline and why does it matter so much?
A: A written definition of "good": what a response must contain, what disqualifies it, and worked examples of good/borderline/bad with reasoning. It matters because writing it forces the team to decide what they actually want, and because the same artifact serves as both the judge prompt and the human annotator instructions — keeping them consistent.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the four-stage model selection workflow?
A: (1) Filter by hard constraints — licence, privacy, deployment, context length. (2) Shortlist with public benchmarks. (3) Evaluate on your own data with your own criteria — the decisive step. (4) Trial in production on a small traffic slice. Cheap filters first.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Name the four latency metrics that matter for LLM serving.
A: TTFT (time to first token — responsiveness, driven by prefill), TPOT (time per output token — driven by memory bandwidth), total latency (TTFT + TPOT × tokens), and throughput (requests/sec, driven by batching).
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the most underrated risk of using a commercial API model?
A: Version instability — providers can change model behaviour without changing the version string, so carefully tuned prompts degrade silently. Pin versions where possible and monitor for drift where not.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why must evaluation results be sliced rather than aggregated?
A: Aggregates hide exactly the failures that matter — a model can be 92% overall and 60% for one user segment, one language, or one query type. Same reasoning as error analysis on a confusion matrix.
