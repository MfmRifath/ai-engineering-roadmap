# H2 — Understanding Foundation Models

> **Thesis:** A model's behaviour is determined by three things you never see — its training data,
> its post-training, and its sampling configuration — and the last one is the only one you
> control.

## Why this chapter matters

It explains *why models behave as they do*, which is what lets you predict failures instead of
discovering them. The sampling section is also the best treatment in the five books of how
probabilistic generation causes the problems everyone complains about.

## Core concepts

### Training data determines the ceiling

**Multilingual performance is a data problem.** English dominates web-scraped corpora, so
under-represented languages get less capable models *and* worse tokenization
([A2](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch02.md)) — a double penalty of lower quality at
higher cost per token. If you build multilingual products, measure per-language, always.

**Domain-specific models** exist for the same reason: general web text under-represents medical,
legal, and scientific language. Specialized pretraining or continued pretraining
([A11](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch11.md)) helps where the gap is real.

### Architecture and size

Transformers dominate, with state-space models (Mamba) the main serious alternative. The
constraints Huyen highlights are the ones you can act on: **quadratic attention** and the **KV
cache** ([A3](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch03.md)).

**Scaling laws** (Chinchilla) say compute should be split between parameters and data — and the
key correction is that **most models were historically under-trained on data**. Practical
consequences:

- Parameter count alone is a poor capability predictor. A well-trained 8B beats a badly trained
  70B.
- The industry now trains smaller models on far more tokens, because *inference* cost dominates
  the lifetime bill. You pay for parameters on every request forever.

**Mixture of Experts** decouples total from active parameters — large capacity, smaller
per-token compute.

### Post-training is where the personality comes from

The base model is a text completer ([A1](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch01.md)). Two
stages make it an assistant:

**1. Supervised fine-tuning** on (instruction, response) demonstrations. Teaches format and task
behaviour. ([R7](../../03-build-llm-from-scratch-raschka/notes/ch07.md) is this, by hand.)

**2. Preference finetuning.** Teaches which of two valid responses humans prefer.

- **RLHF** — train a reward model on human preference comparisons, then optimize the policy
  against it with PPO plus a KL penalty to the reference model
  ([G18](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch18.md)).
- **DPO** — optimize the preference objective directly, no reward model, no RL loop. Simpler and
  now dominant in open work.

Huyen's important warning: **the reward model is a proxy**, and optimizing hard against a proxy
finds its flaws. That is reward hacking, and it explains observed behaviours like excessive
hedging, sycophancy, and verbosity — the reward model rewarded them.

### Sampling — where non-determinism lives

The model outputs a probability distribution; sampling picks from it. This is *the* source of
LLM inconsistency, and it is worth stating plainly: **the model is not confused, it is sampling.**

Beyond temperature/top-k/top-p ([A6](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch06.md)), Huyen adds
the ones that matter in production:

- **Logprobs** — the model's actual confidence. Enormously useful and under-used: you can flag
  low-confidence outputs for review, detect out-of-distribution inputs, and build a threshold
  where a generative classifier otherwise gives you none ([A4](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch04.md)).
- **Beam search** — maintain *k* candidate sequences. Standard for translation, generally poor
  for open-ended generation (it produces bland, high-probability text).
- **Stop sequences** — bound cost and prevent runaway generation.

### Test-time compute

Spend more inference compute for better answers: sample *n* and pick the best, or let the model
reason at length before answering.

**Best-of-n needs a selector**, and the selection is the hard part. Huyen notes the statistical
trap: **picking the max over noisy estimates is biased upward** — the same bias Double DQN
corrects ([G18](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch18.md)). If your scorer is noisy, best-of-n
overestimates the quality it delivers.

`[dated]` Reasoning models (o-series, extended thinking, R1) internalize this via RL, trading
latency and tokens for accuracy. The engineering question is now *when* the trade is worth it,
per request.

### Structured outputs

Three approaches, in increasing strength: prompting, fine-tuning, and **constrained decoding**,
which masks invalid tokens at each step so invalid output is unreachable
([A6](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch06.md)).

Huyen's caution is worth keeping: constraints guarantee *syntactic* validity, not *semantic*
correctness. Valid JSON with a hallucinated field value is still wrong. Validate the content.

### Hallucination, explained rather than lamented

Two mechanisms:

1. **The model samples plausible continuations.** It has no truth predicate. Fluency and accuracy
   are separate properties, and the training objective optimizes only the first.
2. **Training taught it to answer.** Post-training rewards helpful responses, so "I don't know"
   is under-rewarded relative to a confident guess.

Mitigations, in order of effectiveness: **ground it** (RAG, [H6](ch06.md)), **give it an explicit
escape hatch** ("say you don't know"), **verify** (functional checks, citations, cross-checking),
and **use logprobs** to flag low-confidence spans.

What does *not* work: telling it not to hallucinate.

## Key code

```python
# Logprobs: the most under-used production signal
resp = client.chat.completions.create(
    model=MODEL, messages=messages,
    logprobs=True, top_logprobs=5,
    temperature=0,
)
tokens = resp.choices[0].logprobs.content
avg_logprob = sum(t.logprob for t in tokens) / len(tokens)
if avg_logprob < THRESHOLD:
    route_to_human_review(resp)      # a threshold where you otherwise had none
```

```python
# Structured output: guaranteed syntax, unguaranteed semantics
from pydantic import BaseModel

class Extraction(BaseModel):
    company: str
    amount_usd: float
    date: str

result = client.beta.chat.completions.parse(
    model=MODEL, messages=messages, response_format=Extraction,
)
# Schema-valid. Now check the values are actually right.
```

## Gotchas

- **Blaming the model for non-determinism.** It is sampling. Set temperature 0 for extraction and
  classification.
- **Expecting temperature 0 to be bit-reproducible.** Batched GPU float arithmetic says otherwise.
- **Judging capability by parameter count.** Training tokens and post-training matter more.
- **Ignoring per-language performance.** Multilingual products need per-language evaluation.
- **Trusting structured output's content.** Syntax is guaranteed; semantics are not.
- **Best-of-n with a noisy selector.** Max-of-noisy-estimates is biased upward; you will
  overestimate the quality you shipped.
- **Instructing the model not to hallucinate.** Ground it and give it an escape hatch instead.
- **Ignoring logprobs.** They are free confidence signal, available on most APIs.

## Exercises

**Understand**
1. Why do under-represented languages get a double penalty?
2. What do scaling laws imply about choosing a model in 2026?
3. Explain reward hacking and give two behaviours it plausibly explains.
4. Give the two mechanisms behind hallucination and the mitigations for each.

**Build**
1. Run the same prompt 20 times at temperature 0 and at 0.7. Measure output variance both ways.
2. Extract logprobs for correct and incorrect answers on a task with known ground truth. Plot the
   distributions and find a usable threshold. **This is a production-grade technique very few
   teams use.**
3. Implement best-of-5 with an LLM scorer, then hand-rate the outputs. Measure whether the
   selected answer is really the best — and quantify the upward bias.
4. Compare a schema-constrained extraction against a prompt-only one on 100 documents. Count
   parse failures and, separately, semantic errors.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: Why do under-represented languages get worse LLM performance *and* higher cost?
A: Training corpora are English-dominated, so the model is less capable in those languages; and the tokenizer's vocabulary was learned from that same corpus, so the same text fragments into 3–5× more tokens. Lower quality at higher price.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What do scaling laws (Chinchilla) imply for model selection?
A: Compute should be split between parameters and training tokens, and most historical models were under-trained on data. So parameter count is a poor capability predictor — a well-trained 8B can beat a badly trained 70B — and the industry now favours smaller models trained longer, because inference cost dominates the lifetime bill.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is reward hacking in RLHF, and what behaviours does it explain?
A: The reward model is a proxy for human preference, and optimizing hard against a proxy finds its flaws rather than the intent. It plausibly explains sycophancy, excessive hedging, and verbosity — the reward model scored those highly.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What are the two mechanisms behind hallucination?
A: (1) The model samples plausible continuations and has no truth predicate — fluency and accuracy are separate properties. (2) Post-training rewards being helpful, so a confident guess is better rewarded than "I don't know."
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What are logprobs good for in production?
A: They are the model's confidence, available for free on most APIs. Use them to flag low-confidence outputs for human review, detect out-of-distribution inputs, and create a decision threshold where a generative classifier otherwise gives you none.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What does constrained decoding guarantee, and what does it not?
A: It guarantees syntactic validity — the output will parse against the schema, because invalid tokens are masked and unreachable. It guarantees nothing about semantic correctness: a schema-valid object with hallucinated values is still wrong.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the statistical trap in best-of-n selection?
A: Taking the max over noisy estimates is biased upward. If the selector is imperfect, the chosen sample's *measured* quality overstates its true quality — the same bias Double DQN was invented to correct.
