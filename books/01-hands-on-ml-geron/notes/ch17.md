# G17 — Autoencoders and GANs

> **Thesis:** Force a network to reconstruct its input through a narrow bottleneck and it must
> learn what the data actually *is* — a compressed, structured representation nobody labeled.

## Why this chapter matters

Marked **Context** in the roadmap, but three ideas here explain things you will otherwise take on
faith:

1. **Latent space** — the concrete version of "embedding space." Autoencoders let you *see* that
   a learned representation has structure, because you can decode points from it.
2. **Self-supervision through reconstruction** — the same idea as masked language modeling.
   Corrupt the input, predict the original, learn representations for free.
3. **Adversarial training** — one network learning against another. Its lineage runs through
   RLHF's reward models and through adversarial evaluation of LLMs.

## Core concepts

### Autoencoders

An encoder compresses input to a **latent representation** (**codings**); a decoder reconstructs.
Train on **reconstruction loss** — the input is the label, so it is self-supervised.

The **bottleneck** is the whole mechanism. Make the latent dimension smaller than the input and
the network must discard something; what it keeps is what matters. Such an autoencoder is
**undercomplete**, which is what forces it to learn rather than copy.

**A linear autoencoder with MSE loss performs PCA** ([G8](ch08.md)). That equivalence is the
cleanest bridge between classical and neural representation learning: adding nonlinear layers
gives you *nonlinear* PCA, capable of unrolling manifolds a linear projection cannot.

**Stacked autoencoders** add depth, usually symmetrically. **Tying weights** — the decoder reuses
the encoder's transposed weights — halves the parameters and regularizes.

**Convolutional autoencoders** for images; **recurrent** for sequences.

**Greedy layerwise pretraining** — train one shallow autoencoder at a time and stack them —
was historically how deep networks got trained before good initialization and normalization
existed. Mostly a historical note now, but a good reminder that "how do we train this at all" was
once the hard part.

### Regularized autoencoders: bottleneck by other means

You can force learning without making the layer narrow:

- **Denoising autoencoders** — corrupt the input (Gaussian noise or dropout), reconstruct the
  *clean* original. The network cannot copy, because the copy would include the noise.

  > **This is masked language modeling.** BERT corrupts by masking tokens and reconstructs the
  > originals. Same principle, different corruption. It is also the ancestor of the "predict what
  > was removed" family of self-supervised objectives.

- **Sparse autoencoders** — penalize activations so only a few units fire per input, via an ℓ1
  penalty or a KL divergence against a target sparsity. Each unit must specialize.

  > `[dated]` **Sparse autoencoders became central to LLM interpretability after this edition.**
  > Anthropic and others train SAEs on transformer activations to decompose superposed neurons
  > into monosemantic features. If you ever read modern interpretability work, this section is
  > the prerequisite.

### Variational autoencoders

Both probabilistic and generative. The encoder outputs a **mean μ and log-variance γ**, not a
point; the coding is sampled from that Gaussian.

The loss has two terms:
1. **Reconstruction loss** — the usual.
2. **Latent loss** (KL divergence) — pushes the codings toward a standard Gaussian.

The KL term is what makes the latent space *usable*. Without it, the encoder scatters codings
arbitrarily and the space between them decodes to nothing. With it, the space is smooth and
continuous, so you can sample randomly and get valid outputs, and interpolate between two points
to get a smooth morph.

**Semantic interpolation** in VAE latent space — smoothly morphing one face into another — is the
most vivid demonstration in the chapter that a learned representation carries meaning. Keep it in
mind when [A2](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch02.md) shows you word-embedding arithmetic.

The **reparameterization trick** (sample ε ~ N(0,1), then compute μ + σ·ε) is what makes the
sampling differentiable, so gradients can flow through a random node.

### GANs

A **generator** turns noise into fakes; a **discriminator** classifies real vs fake. They train in
alternating phases, each getting better because the other does. Géron's framing — a counterfeiter
versus a detective — is apt.

GANs are notoriously hard to train:

- **Mode collapse** — the generator finds one output that fools the discriminator and emits only
  that, losing all diversity.
- **Nash equilibrium instability** — the two networks can oscillate rather than converge, and the
  training loss tells you almost nothing about sample quality.
- **Parameter sensitivity** — small changes destabilize everything.

Mitigations: experience replay, mini-batch discrimination, and **DCGAN**'s architectural rules
(strided convolutions rather than pooling, BatchNorm, no fully-connected hidden layers, ReLU in
the generator except tanh at the output). **Progressive growing** and **StyleGAN** add resolution
gradually and inject style at multiple scales.

`[dated]` **Diffusion models** — which postdate this edition — have largely replaced GANs for
image generation. They are more stable to train and produce better samples. But the adversarial
*idea* persists: reward models in RLHF are trained on preferences and then optimized against,
which is structurally the same dynamic, with the same instability risks.

## Key code

```python
# Stacked autoencoder: encoder and decoder as separate models, joined
encoder = keras.Sequential([
    keras.layers.Flatten(input_shape=[28, 28]),
    keras.layers.Dense(100, activation="selu"),
    keras.layers.Dense(30, activation="selu"),      # the bottleneck
])
decoder = keras.Sequential([
    keras.layers.Dense(100, activation="selu", input_shape=[30]),
    keras.layers.Dense(28 * 28, activation="sigmoid"),
    keras.layers.Reshape([28, 28]),
])
ae = keras.Sequential([encoder, decoder])
ae.compile(loss="binary_crossentropy", optimizer="nadam")
ae.fit(X_train, X_train, epochs=20)      # note: X is both input AND target

# Denoising: corrupt the input, reconstruct the clean original
denoising = keras.Sequential([keras.layers.GaussianNoise(0.2), encoder, decoder])

# VAE reparameterization trick — makes sampling differentiable
class Sampling(keras.layers.Layer):
    def call(self, inputs):
        mean, log_var = inputs
        return keras.backend.random_normal(tf.shape(log_var)) * keras.backend.exp(log_var / 2) + mean
```

## Gotchas

- **An overcomplete autoencoder with no regularization.** If the latent layer is as wide as the
  input, the network learns the identity function and you have learned nothing. Bottleneck or
  regularize.
- **Reconstructing the noisy input in a denoising autoencoder.** The *target* must be the clean
  original. Getting this backwards defeats the entire method.
- **Omitting the KL term in a VAE.** You get an ordinary autoencoder with a useless latent space —
  sampling from it produces garbage.
- **Sampling without the reparameterization trick.** Gradients cannot flow through a random node.
- **Reading GAN loss as progress.** Discriminator and generator losses hovering near equilibrium
  says nothing about sample quality. Look at samples, or use FID.
- **Mode collapse mistaken for success.** Sharp, convincing outputs that are all nearly identical.
  Always inspect diversity.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does an autoencoder need a bottleneck (or equivalent regularization)?
A: Without one it can learn the identity function and copy input to output, learning nothing. Constraining capacity forces it to discard information, and what it chooses to keep is the useful representation.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What classical technique does a linear autoencoder with MSE loss reproduce?
A: PCA. Adding nonlinear layers generalizes it to nonlinear dimensionality reduction, capable of unrolling manifolds a linear projection cannot.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is a denoising autoencoder, and which famous LLM training objective is it?
A: Corrupt the input (noise or dropout) and train to reconstruct the *clean* original. BERT's masked language modeling is exactly this — masking is the corruption, and the original tokens are the target.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What does the KL divergence term do in a VAE?
A: It pushes the encoded distributions toward a standard Gaussian, making the latent space smooth and continuous. Without it, codings scatter arbitrarily and points between them decode to nothing — so you cannot sample or interpolate.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the reparameterization trick and why is it needed?
A: Sample ε ~ N(0,1) and compute μ + σ·ε instead of sampling directly from N(μ, σ). It moves the randomness outside the computation graph so gradients can flow through what would otherwise be a non-differentiable random node.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is mode collapse in a GAN?
A: The generator discovers one (or a few) outputs that reliably fool the discriminator and produces only those, losing all diversity. The loss curves can look healthy throughout, which is why you must inspect samples.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why are sparse autoencoders suddenly relevant to LLM interpretability?
A: Trained on transformer activations, they decompose superposed polysemantic neurons into sparse, more monosemantic features — the main current technique for reading what an LLM has internally represented.
<!-- /card -->
