# G8 — Dimensionality Reduction

> **Thesis:** High-dimensional space is deeply unintuitive and mostly empty — but real data
> almost always lies on a much lower-dimensional surface inside it, and finding that surface is
> both a compression trick and the conceptual root of embeddings.

## Why this chapter matters

This is where "embedding" becomes a real idea rather than a buzzword. When
[A2](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch02.md) tells you a token is a 768-dimensional vector
and that semantically similar tokens are near each other, the machinery for believing that comes
from here: the **manifold hypothesis**, distance behaviour in high dimensions, and the projection
techniques (UMAP/t-SNE) that every embedding visualization you will ever see depends on.

Chapter 5 of Alammar's book — clustering documents by embedding, reducing with UMAP, then topic
modeling — is this chapter applied to text.

## Core concepts

### The curse of dimensionality

High-dimensional space behaves nothing like the 2D and 3D intuitions you have:

- Pick a random point in a unit square: ~0.4% chance it is within 0.001 of a border. In a
  10,000-dimensional hypercube: **>99.999999%**. Nearly all points are on the boundary.
- Two random points in a unit square average ~0.52 apart. In a 1,000,000-dimensional hypercube:
  **~408.25**. Despite the space having the same unit "width."

**Consequence:** in high dimensions, everything is far from everything else, and all distances
become similar. Nearest-neighbour methods degrade badly, and any new instance is likely far from
every training example — so predictions are extrapolations. **More dimensions → exponentially
more data needed for the same density.**

> This is why cosine similarity, not Euclidean distance, dominates embedding search
> ([A8](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch08.md)). Angles stay informative in high
> dimensions where magnitudes stop being.

### The manifold hypothesis

The saving grace: real data is not uniformly distributed through its space. Images of faces
occupy a vanishingly small, highly structured region of all possible pixel arrays. A
*d*-dimensional manifold is a region of *n*-dimensional space (*d* < *n*) that locally resembles
*d*-dimensional space — the Swiss roll being the standard picture.

**The implicit second assumption:** the task will be simpler when expressed on the manifold. This
is often true and **not always** — Géron shows a counterexample where unrolling makes the
decision boundary harder. Dimensionality reduction is not a free lunch; measure it.

### PCA

Find the axis that preserves the **maximum variance** (equivalently, minimizes mean squared
distance to the projection), then the orthogonal axis preserving the most remaining variance, and
so on. Those are the **principal components**, obtained via SVD:

$$X = U \Sigma V^\top$$

with the components in the rows of $V^\top$. **You must center the data first** — scikit-learn's
`PCA` does it for you; a hand-rolled SVD does not.

Pick the number of components by **explained variance**, not by guesswork:

```python
pca = PCA()
pca.fit(X_train)
d = np.argmax(np.cumsum(pca.explained_variance_ratio_) >= 0.95) + 1

# or, more directly — a float means "preserve this fraction of variance"
pca = PCA(n_components=0.95)
X_reduced = pca.fit_transform(X_train)
```

Look for an **elbow** in the cumulative-variance curve. MNIST compresses from 784 → ~150
dimensions at 95% variance retained.

PCA is **lossy but invertible-ish**: `inverse_transform` reconstructs an approximation, and the
mean squared distance to the original is the *reconstruction error*. Viewing reconstructed MNIST
digits is the most intuitive demonstration in the chapter.

**Variants:**

| Variant | Use |
|---|---|
| **Randomized PCA** | *O(m d²)* rather than *O(m n²)* — much faster when *d* ≪ *n*. Default when it applies. |
| **Incremental PCA** | Mini-batches; supports out-of-core and `partial_fit` |
| **Kernel PCA** | The kernel trick from ch. 5 applied to PCA — nonlinear projections |

Selecting a kernel for kPCA is a nice trick: since it is unsupervised, put it in a pipeline before
a classifier and let `GridSearchCV` pick the kernel by **downstream supervised performance**.

### Other techniques

- **LLE** — nonlinear manifold learning that preserves *local* relationships. Unrolls a Swiss
  roll well; scales badly ($O(m^2)$-ish).
- **MDS** — preserves pairwise distances.
- **Isomap** — preserves geodesic (along-the-manifold) distances.
- **t-SNE** — for **visualization only**. Keeps similar points close and dissimilar points apart.
- **LDA** — supervised; projects to maximize *class* separation. A good preprocessing step before
  another classifier.

`[dated]` **UMAP** postdates this edition and has largely displaced t-SNE: faster, better global
structure preservation, and it can `transform` new points (t-SNE cannot). Alammar uses UMAP
throughout chapter 5. Learn UMAP.

**Critical warning on t-SNE and UMAP:** cluster *sizes* and inter-cluster *distances* in these
plots are not meaningful. They are for spotting structure, never for measuring it. People
routinely over-read these pictures.

## Key code

```python
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA, IncrementalPCA

# Preserve 95% of variance, let PCA choose the dimensionality
pca = PCA(n_components=0.95, svd_solver="randomized", random_state=42)
X_reduced = pca.fit_transform(X_train)
print(f"{X_train.shape[1]} -> {pca.n_components_} dims")

# Out-of-core, for data that doesn't fit in memory
inc = IncrementalPCA(n_components=154)
for batch in np.array_split(X_train, 100):
    inc.partial_fit(batch)
```

## Gotchas

- **Fitting PCA on train+test.** Same leakage rule as every other transformer: `fit_transform`
  on train, `transform` on test.
- **Forgetting to scale first.** PCA maximizes variance, so an unscaled feature measured in
  millimetres will dominate every component. Scale unless the units are already comparable.
- **Over-reading t-SNE/UMAP plots.** Cluster size and inter-cluster distance are artifacts.
- **Assuming reduction always helps.** It loses information and sometimes makes the task harder.
  Measure downstream performance, do not assume.
- **Using PCA when the structure is nonlinear.** PCA is a linear projection. For a Swiss roll it
  flattens rather than unrolls. Use kernel PCA, LLE, or UMAP.
- **Reducing dimensions to fix overfitting.** Sometimes it works, but regularization is usually
  the more direct tool.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: State the curse of dimensionality with a concrete number.
A: Two random points in a unit square are ~0.52 apart on average; in a 1,000,000-dimensional unit hypercube, ~408.25. In high dimensions everything is far from everything, distances stop discriminating, and nearly all points sit near the boundary.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the manifold hypothesis?
A: Real high-dimensional data concentrates on a much lower-dimensional manifold — images of faces occupy a tiny structured region of all possible pixel arrays. The implicit second assumption, that the task is simpler on the manifold, is often but not always true.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: How does PCA choose its axes?
A: The first component is the direction of maximum variance (equivalently, minimum squared projection distance); each subsequent component is orthogonal to the previous ones and captures the most remaining variance. Computed via SVD, on **centered** data.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: How do you choose the number of PCA components?
A: By explained variance, not intuition: take the smallest d whose cumulative `explained_variance_ratio_` reaches your target (commonly 95%). In scikit-learn, `PCA(n_components=0.95)` does it directly.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What must you never conclude from a t-SNE or UMAP plot?
A: That cluster sizes or the distances between clusters mean anything. Both are artifacts of the algorithm. These plots reveal that structure exists; they do not measure it.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does embedding search use cosine similarity rather than Euclidean distance?
A: In high dimensions Euclidean distances concentrate — everything is roughly equidistant — while angular relationships stay informative. Cosine also ignores magnitude, which for embeddings usually encodes frequency rather than meaning.
<!-- /card -->
