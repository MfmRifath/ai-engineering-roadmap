# G7 — Ensemble Learning and Random Forests

> **Thesis:** A crowd of weak, *diverse*, independent-ish models beats a single strong one — and
> the entire art is manufacturing the diversity.

## Why this chapter matters

Gradient-boosted trees are still the best general tool for tabular data in 2026, full stop. And
the underlying principle — aggregate diverse predictors to cancel individual errors — reappears
directly as **self-consistency** in LLM prompting
([A6](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch06.md)) and as **ensembles of judges** in
LLM evaluation ([H3](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch03.md)). Sample the model several
times, take the majority answer. It is voting, rediscovered.

## Core concepts

### Why ensembles work: the biased-coin argument

Géron's framing is the clearest available. A coin with 51% heads, flipped 1,000 times, gives >50%
heads about **75%** of the time. Flip it 10,000 times and that rises to **97%**. The law of large
numbers turns a tiny edge into near-certainty.

Same with classifiers: 1,000 predictors each 51% accurate, voted, can reach ~75%+.

**The catch, and the entire subject of the chapter:** this requires the errors to be
**independent**. Models trained on the same data with the same algorithm make the *same* mistakes,
so their votes are correlated and the gain evaporates. Every technique below is a different
answer to "how do I make these models disagree?"

### Voting: hard vs soft

- **Hard voting** — majority of predicted labels.
- **Soft voting** — average of predicted probabilities, then argmax. Usually better, because it
  weights confident votes more. Requires every estimator to expose `predict_proba` (for `SVC`,
  set `probability=True`).

Diversity source: **different algorithms**.

### Bagging and pasting

Same algorithm, different random subsets of the training data.

- **Bagging** (bootstrap aggregating) — sample **with** replacement
- **Pasting** — sample **without** replacement

Bagging introduces more diversity per predictor (each sees a more distorted sample), so it gives
slightly **higher bias but lower variance**. Géron: bagging is generally preferred, but
cross-validate if you can afford it.

Predictors train **in parallel** — bagging scales trivially across cores and machines.

**Out-of-bag evaluation** is the elegant bonus. With bootstrap sampling, each predictor sees only
~63% of the training instances ($1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632$). The other 37% are its **oob**
instances, and averaging each predictor's oob performance gives you a free validation score —
no separate holdout needed. `BaggingClassifier(oob_score=True)`.

### Random forests = bagging + random feature subsets

A random forest bags decision trees *and* restricts each split to a random subset of features
(`max_features`, default √n for classification). That is a second, independent source of
diversity: trees now disagree about *which features to use*, not just which rows they saw.

**Extra-Trees** (Extremely Randomized Trees) push further: thresholds are chosen **at random**
rather than searched for the best. Much faster to train (the threshold search is the expensive
part), higher bias, lower variance. Géron's honest advice: you cannot predict which of RF and
Extra-Trees will win — cross-validate.

**Feature importance** comes free: average, across all trees, how much each feature's splits
reduced impurity, weighted by the samples they touched. Fast and useful for feature selection —
but see the gotcha about correlated features.

### Boosting: sequential, not parallel

Train predictors **one after another**, each correcting its predecessor. This is the fundamental
trade: boosting usually beats bagging on accuracy but **cannot be parallelized**, because
predictor *n+1* needs predictor *n*'s results.

**AdaBoost** — after each round, *increase the weights* of misclassified instances so the next
predictor focuses on them. Each predictor gets a say proportional to its accuracy. If it overfits,
reduce `n_estimators` or regularize the base estimator.

**Gradient Boosting** — instead of reweighting instances, fit each new predictor to the
**residual errors** of the ensemble so far. Simple and powerful.

The critical hyperparameter is **`learning_rate`** (shrinkage). Low values (~0.1) mean each tree
contributes less, so you need more trees — but the ensemble generalizes better. This is the
classic **learning rate ↓ / n_estimators ↑** trade, and it is the same tradeoff you will meet in
LLM fine-tuning.

Find `n_estimators` by **early stopping** rather than guessing:

```python
gbrt = GradientBoostingRegressor(max_depth=2, n_estimators=120)
gbrt.fit(X_train, y_train)
errors = [mean_squared_error(y_val, p) for p in gbrt.staged_predict(X_val)]
best_n = np.argmin(errors) + 1
```

`staged_predict` yields predictions after each stage — the whole error curve in one fit.

`[dated]` The book recommends **XGBoost**, still excellent. **LightGBM** and **CatBoost** are now
equally standard, and scikit-learn's own `HistGradientBoostingClassifier` is fast, built in, and
handles missing values natively. Reach for that first.

### Stacking

Instead of a hard-coded aggregation rule, **train a model to aggregate**. The base predictors'
outputs become the input features to a **blender** (meta-learner), trained on a held-out fold to
avoid leakage. Best performance, most complexity, most ways to leak. `StackingClassifier`.

## Key code

```python
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier, HistGradientBoostingClassifier

# n_jobs=-1: bagging is embarrassingly parallel. Boosting is not.
rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=500, max_leaf_nodes=16,
                            oob_score=True, n_jobs=-1, random_state=42)
rf.fit(X_train, y_train)
print(rf.oob_score_)                       # free validation estimate

for name, score in sorted(zip(names, rf.feature_importances_),
                          key=lambda t: -t[1])[:10]:
    print(f"{score:.3f}  {name}")

# The modern default for tabular data
hgb = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(learning_rate=0.1, max_iter=500,
                                     early_stopping=True, random_state=42)
```

## Gotchas

- **Ensembling correlated models.** Five variants of the same gradient-boosted tree is not an
  ensemble, it is one model with extra latency. Diversity is the whole mechanism.
- **Soft voting without `probability=True` on `SVC`.** It will not have `predict_proba`.
- **Expecting boosting to parallelize.** It cannot, by construction. `n_jobs` will not help.
- **Too many boosting rounds.** Unlike bagging, boosting *does* overfit with more estimators.
  Use early stopping.
- **Trusting `feature_importances_` with correlated features.** Importance is split arbitrarily
  among correlated columns, making each look unimportant. Use permutation importance.
- **Leaking in stacking.** The blender must train on out-of-fold predictions. Use
  `StackingClassifier`, do not hand-roll it.
- **Reading oob_score as a test score.** It is a validation estimate. You still need a test set.

3. What does out-of-bag evaluation measure, and why is it free?

   accuracy and training time.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: Why do ensembles work, and what is the necessary condition?
A: Law of large numbers — many slightly-better-than-random voters aggregate to a much more accurate one. The condition is **error independence**: models must make *different* mistakes. Correlated models give no benefit.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Bagging vs pasting?
A: Bagging samples training subsets **with** replacement, pasting **without**. Bagging gives each predictor a more distorted view, so slightly higher bias but lower variance — usually preferred.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is out-of-bag (oob) evaluation?
A: With bootstrap sampling each predictor sees only ~63% of instances (1 − 1/e). The unseen ~37% serve as a validation set for that predictor; averaging across the ensemble gives a free validation score with no separate holdout.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: How does a random forest add diversity beyond bagging?
A: It also restricts each split to a random subset of features (`max_features`, default √n). Trees then disagree about which features to use, not just which rows they saw.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: AdaBoost vs gradient boosting — what does each new predictor learn from?
A: AdaBoost re-weights the *instances* the previous predictors got wrong. Gradient boosting fits the new predictor directly to the *residual errors* of the ensemble so far.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why can't boosting be parallelized?
A: It is sequential by definition — predictor n+1 is trained on the errors (or reweighted data) produced by predictor n. Bagging, by contrast, trains every predictor independently and scales across cores trivially.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What LLM technique is ensemble voting in disguise?
A: **Self-consistency** — sample the same prompt several times at nonzero temperature and take the majority answer. Also ensembles of LLM judges in evaluation.
<!-- /card -->
