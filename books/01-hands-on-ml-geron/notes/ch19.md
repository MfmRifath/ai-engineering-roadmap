# G19 — Training and Deploying at Scale

> **Thesis:** A model that is not served is not a product, and serving imposes constraints —
> latency, cost, versioning, monitoring — that have nothing to do with accuracy.

## Why this chapter matters

This is the only chapter in Géron that is about **operations**, and it is the direct predecessor
of [H9](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch09.md) and
[H10](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch10.md). Batching, versioning, quantization, hardware
utilization, and monitoring all reappear there at LLM scale with harder numbers.

`[dated]` TF Serving, GCP AI Platform, and `tf.distribute` specifics have moved. The
**concerns** — how do I version, batch, scale, and watch this thing — are permanent.

## Core concepts

### Serving a model as a service, not a library

Wrapping a model in its own service (rather than embedding it in your app) buys you: independent
scaling, independent deployment, language independence, and **version management**. The cost is a
network hop and an extra thing to operate.

**The SavedModel format** bundles the computation graph *and* the weights *and* the preprocessing
layers. That last point matters — it is the fix for training/serving skew from
[G13](ch13.md). Ship preprocessing with the model or it will drift.

**Versioning and gradual rollout.** TF Serving watches a directory and hot-swaps versions when new
subdirectories appear. The pattern generalizes:

- **Canary** — route a small percentage of traffic to the new version, watch metrics, ramp up.
- **A/B test** — split traffic deliberately to compare.
- **Shadow** — send production traffic to the new version but discard its responses, comparing
  offline. The safest, and underused.

You keep the old version loaded so rollback is instant. This is exactly what Huyen's model
**router and gateway** does in [H10](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch10.md).

**Batching at the server.** Group concurrent requests into one batch before running inference —
better hardware utilization at the cost of a little latency. The tuning knobs (max batch size,
max wait time) are precisely the ones that reappear as **continuous batching** in LLM serving.

### gRPC vs REST

REST/JSON is convenient and human-debuggable. gRPC with protobufs is much faster for large
tensors, because JSON serializes floats as text. For big inputs the difference is large. LLM
serving mostly uses HTTP+SSE for streaming, but the underlying tradeoff — human-readable vs
compact-and-fast — is the same decision.

### Deploying to mobile and embedded

**TFLite** shrinks and accelerates models by:

1. Freezing the graph and pruning training-only operations.
2. Optimizing computations (fusing operations, folding constants).
3. **Post-training quantization** — storing weights as 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floats.

**Quantization is 4× smaller and often faster**, at a small accuracy cost. The mechanics: store a
scale and zero-point per tensor, map floats onto integers, dequantize on use.

> **This section is more important than it looks.** Everything in
> [H7](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch07.md) and
> [H9](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch09.md) about int8, int4, and GGUF quantization for
> LLMs is this idea scaled up. When you load a "4-bit quantized 7B model" on a laptop, this is
> the mechanism. **Quantization-aware training** — simulating quantization during training so the
> model adapts — is also introduced here and is the ancestor of QLoRA.

### Using GPUs

Practical concerns that all transfer:

- **Memory is the binding constraint.** By default TF grabs all GPU memory; limit it or enable
  growth so processes can share.
- **Operations are placed** on GPU where a kernel exists, else CPU. Watch for silent CPU
  fallbacks — they destroy throughput and produce no error.
- Multiple processes on one GPU need explicit memory limits.

### Distributed training

Two axes:

**Data parallelism** — replicate the model on each device, split the batch, average the gradients.
The common case.

- **Mirrored strategy** — synchronous. Every replica computes gradients, an **AllReduce** averages
  them, all replicas apply the same update. Simple and correct; the slowest worker sets the pace
  (the *straggler* problem).
- **Parameter server** — asynchronous. Replicas fetch parameters, compute, push updates. No
  stragglers, but **stale gradients**: a replica computes on parameters that have since changed.
  Géron notes async often underperforms in practice for this reason.

**Model parallelism** — split the *model* across devices. Hard, because layers depend on each
other and the split creates communication. Géron's verdict — usually not worth it — was true for
CNNs. `[dated]` For LLMs it became mandatory: a 70B model does not fit on one GPU. Modern training
combines **tensor parallelism** (split a layer's matrices), **pipeline parallelism** (split by
layers), and **data parallelism** — 3D parallelism. And **ZeRO/FSDP** shard optimizer states,
gradients, and parameters across devices to cut redundant memory.

**Large batch training** needs care: increasing batch size without adapting the learning rate
usually hurts. Warmup and linear LR scaling are the standard fixes — the same warmup you meet in
[G11](ch11.md) and in Raschka's Appendix D.

## Key code

```python
# SavedModel: graph + weights + preprocessing, versioned by directory
model_version = "0001"
model_path = os.path.join("my_model", model_version)
tf.saved_model.save(model, model_path)

# Post-training quantization: 4x smaller, usually faster, small accuracy cost
converter = tf.lite.TFLiteConverter.from_saved_model(model_path)
converter.optimizations = [tf.lite.Optimize.DEFAULT]
tflite_model = converter.convert()

# Data parallelism: build the model INSIDE the strategy scope
distribution = tf.distribute.MirroredStrategy()
with distribution.scope():
    model = create_model()
    model.compile(loss="sparse_categorical_crossentropy", optimizer="nadam")
model.fit(X_train, y_train, epochs=10)     # batch is split across replicas
```

## Gotchas

- **Not shipping preprocessing with the model.** Training/serving skew, silent and slow to find.
- **Building the model outside the distribution scope.** Variables are created on one device and
  the strategy has no effect. No error is raised.
- **Batch size confusion in data parallelism.** The `batch_size` you pass is the *global* batch,
  split across replicas. Each replica sees `batch_size / n_replicas`.
- **Scaling batch size without adjusting the learning rate.** Quality degrades. Use warmup and
  linear scaling.
- **Silent CPU fallback.** An op with no GPU kernel runs on CPU, tanking throughput without
  raising anything. Log device placement when profiling.
- **Assuming async parameter servers are faster.** Stale gradients often make them converge worse
  than synchronous training.
- **Quantizing without measuring.** Accuracy loss is task-dependent. Always evaluate the
  quantized model, never assume.
- **No rollback plan.** Keep the previous version loaded and routable.

## Exercises

**Understand**
1. Why deploy a model as a service instead of embedding it in the application?
2. What does quantization trade, and by what mechanism does it save memory?
3. Mirrored vs parameter-server strategy — what does each trade?
4. Why does large-batch training need learning-rate warmup?

**Build**
1. Export a model as SavedModel, serve it, then deploy a second version alongside and shift
   traffic gradually (Géron ex. 10).
2. Quantize a trained model with TFLite. Measure size, latency, and accuracy before and after,
   and record all three. This is the workflow you will repeat for LLMs.
3. Train the same model on one GPU vs the mirrored strategy. Measure the actual speedup and
   compare it to the ideal — the gap is communication overhead.
4. Write out the full deployment checklist you would want for
   [p1](../../../projects/p1-end-to-end-ml-service/): versioning, rollback, monitoring, alerting,
   retraining trigger.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: What does a SavedModel contain, and why does that matter?
A: The computation graph, the weights, *and* any preprocessing layers. Bundling preprocessing is what prevents training/serving skew — the transformation ships with the weights instead of being reimplemented in the serving code.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is post-training quantization and what does it buy?
A: Storing weights as 8-bit integers (with a per-tensor scale and zero-point) instead of 32-bit floats. Roughly 4× smaller and often faster, at a small accuracy cost. This is the same mechanism behind 4-bit quantized LLMs.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Mirrored strategy vs parameter server — what's the tradeoff?
A: Mirrored is synchronous: all replicas AllReduce their gradients and apply identical updates — correct, but the slowest worker sets the pace. Parameter server is asynchronous: no stragglers, but replicas compute on stale parameters, which often converges worse.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why was model parallelism "usually not worth it" for CNNs but mandatory for LLMs?
A: CNNs fit on one device, so splitting them added communication for no benefit. A 70B-parameter model simply does not fit, so tensor, pipeline, and data parallelism are combined out of necessity — plus ZeRO/FSDP sharding of optimizer state.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What are canary, A/B, and shadow deployments?
A: Canary routes a small traffic percentage to the new version and ramps up on good metrics. A/B splits traffic deliberately to compare. Shadow sends real traffic to the new version but discards its responses, comparing offline — the safest option.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does server-side batching help, and what does it cost?
A: Grouping concurrent requests into one inference call greatly improves hardware utilization. It costs a little latency, since early requests wait for the batch to fill. Max batch size and max wait time are the knobs — and this is the direct ancestor of continuous batching in LLM serving.
