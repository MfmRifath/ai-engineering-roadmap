# G9 — Unsupervised Learning Techniques

> **Thesis:** Most data in the world is unlabeled, so the techniques that find structure without
> labels — clustering, density estimation, anomaly detection — are the ones that scale.

## Why this chapter matters

Two things carry forward. First, **clustering over embeddings** is exactly what
[A5](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch05.md) does to discover topics in a document
collection — BERTopic is embed → UMAP → HDBSCAN → label, and three of those four are from this
chapter. Second, **anomaly detection** is the shape of most LLM guardrails: you rarely have
labeled examples of every bad output, but you can flag things that sit far from the normal
distribution.

Géron's opening observation is the one to keep: **unsupervised learning is the vastly larger
share of available data.** Labels are expensive; structure is free.

## Core concepts

### K-Means

Pick *k*, then alternate: assign each point to the nearest centroid, move each centroid to the
mean of its points. Repeat until stable. Guaranteed to converge — **but possibly to a local
optimum**, and the result depends entirely on initialization.

Two fixes, both defaults in scikit-learn:
- **`n_init=10`** — run the whole thing 10 times and keep the best by **inertia** (sum of squared
  distances to assigned centroids).
- **K-Means++** — spread the initial centroids apart probabilistically. Dramatically better than
  random init.

**Hard vs soft clustering:** `predict` gives the assigned cluster; `transform` gives the distance
to *every* centroid. That distance vector is a *k*-dimensional representation of the point —
K-Means as a nonlinear dimensionality reducer, and a surprisingly effective preprocessing step.

**Choosing k.** Inertia always decreases with more clusters, so you cannot minimize it. Two
approaches:
- **Elbow** in the inertia curve — crude but quick.
- **Silhouette score** — $(b - a) / \max(a, b)$ where *a* is mean intra-cluster distance and *b*
  is mean distance to the nearest other cluster. Ranges −1 to +1. Better, and the **silhouette
  diagram** (per-instance silhouettes grouped by cluster) is more informative than the single
  number: it shows you which clusters are junk.

**K-Means fails on:** clusters of varying size, varying density, or non-spherical shape. It also
requires scaling. Running PCA first often helps.

**Uses:** image segmentation by colour, semi-supervised learning (label one representative per
cluster, then **propagate**), and preprocessing. The semi-supervised demo is striking — labeling
50 well-chosen representatives beats labeling 50 random instances by a wide margin. **That
argument is the whole basis of active learning**, and it reappears in
[H8](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch08.md) as annotation strategy.

### DBSCAN

Density-based. For each instance, count neighbours within ε. Instances with at least
`min_samples` neighbours are **core instances**; a cluster is a connected chain of core
instances; anything unreachable is an **anomaly**.

| | K-Means | DBSCAN |
|---|---|---|
| Need to specify k? | **Yes** | No |
| Arbitrary cluster shapes? | No (spherical) | **Yes** |
| Identifies outliers? | No | **Yes** |
| Varying density? | Poorly | Poorly (fixed ε) |
| Has `predict`? | Yes | **No** |

That last row surprises people: DBSCAN has `fit_predict` but no `predict`. To classify new
instances you train a separate classifier (e.g. KNN) on the labeled cluster assignments. Géron
shows the pattern.

`[dated]` **HDBSCAN** — hierarchical DBSCAN — handles varying density and removes the ε
parameter. It is what BERTopic and most modern text clustering use. Learn it alongside DBSCAN.

### Gaussian Mixture Models

Assume the data was generated from a mixture of *k* Gaussians with unknown parameters. Fit with
**Expectation-Maximization**, which generalizes K-Means: E-step assigns *soft* cluster
responsibilities, M-step updates each Gaussian's mean, covariance, and weight.

Unlike K-Means, GMM handles **elliptical clusters of different sizes, shapes, densities, and
orientations** — because it fits full covariance matrices. `covariance_type` (`"full"`, `"tied"`,
`"diag"`, `"spherical"`) trades flexibility against parameters to estimate.

GMM is a **generative model**: `sample()` draws new instances, and `score_samples()` gives the
log probability density — which is what makes it an anomaly detector. Flag anything below a
chosen density percentile.

**Choosing k for GMM**: inertia and silhouette do not apply. Use **BIC** or **AIC**, which
penalize parameter count against likelihood. Or use **Bayesian Gaussian Mixture**, which sets
unnecessary cluster weights to ~zero automatically — you pass an upper bound and it finds the
rest.

### Anomaly vs novelty detection

A distinction worth being precise about:
- **Anomaly detection** — the training set may contain outliers; find them.
- **Novelty detection** — the training set is assumed clean; find things unlike it.

Other algorithms: **Isolation Forest** (outliers are easier to isolate with random splits — very
effective in high dimensions), **One-Class SVM**, **Local Outlier Factor**, and
**PCA reconstruction error** (anomalies reconstruct badly).

## Key code

```python
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, DBSCAN
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=5, n_init=10, random_state=42)
labels = kmeans.fit_predict(X)
print(kmeans.inertia_, silhouette_score(X, labels))

# transform() -> distance to each centroid: a k-dim feature representation
X_dists = kmeans.transform(X)

# GMM as an anomaly detector: flag the lowest-density 2%
gm = GaussianMixture(n_components=3, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(X)
densities = gm.score_samples(X)
anomalies = X[densities < np.percentile(densities, 2)]

# Choose k by BIC, not inertia
bics = [GaussianMixture(n_components=k, n_init=10).fit(X).bic(X) for k in range(1, 11)]
```

## Gotchas

- **Choosing k by minimizing inertia.** Inertia falls monotonically with k; k = n instances gives
  zero. Use the elbow or silhouette.
- **Not scaling before K-Means.** It is a Euclidean-distance method; unscaled features dominate.
- **Expecting `predict` from DBSCAN.** It does not exist. Fit a classifier on the cluster labels.
- **Using K-Means on elongated or nested clusters.** It will cut them wrongly and confidently.
  Use DBSCAN/HDBSCAN or GMM.
- **DBSCAN with varying density.** A single ε cannot serve dense and sparse regions. Use HDBSCAN.
- **Treating cluster assignments as ground truth.** Clusters are a hypothesis. Inspect them.
- **BIC/AIC on non-Gaussian data.** They assume the model family is right.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: Why can't you pick k by minimizing K-Means inertia?
A: Inertia decreases monotonically with k and hits zero when every point is its own cluster. Use the elbow in the inertia curve, or better, the silhouette score / silhouette diagram.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the silhouette coefficient?
A: (b − a) / max(a, b), where a is the mean distance to points in the same cluster and b the mean distance to the nearest other cluster. Ranges −1 (wrong cluster) to +1 (well separated).
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Give three ways DBSCAN differs from K-Means.
A: (1) No need to specify the number of clusters. (2) Finds arbitrarily-shaped clusters, not just spherical ones. (3) Explicitly labels outliers. Also: it has no `predict` method — fit a classifier on its labels to handle new points.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What can a Gaussian Mixture Model do that K-Means cannot?
A: Fit elliptical clusters of differing size, shape, density, and orientation (it estimates full covariance matrices); give soft probabilistic assignments; generate new samples; and provide a density estimate usable for anomaly detection.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: How do you choose the number of components for a GMM?
A: Minimize BIC or AIC, which trade likelihood against parameter count. Alternatively use `BayesianGaussianMixture`, which drives unnecessary component weights to ~0 given an upper bound.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What does clustering have to do with reducing labeling cost?
A: Label one representative instance per cluster and propagate the label to the cluster. Géron's demo shows 50 well-chosen representatives massively outperforming 50 random labels — the core argument for active learning.
<!-- /card -->
