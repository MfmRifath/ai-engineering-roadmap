# G11 — Training Deep Neural Networks

> **Thesis:** Deep networks do not train by default. Every technique in this chapter exists
> because someone hit a wall — and together they are the reason the 2012 breakthrough happened
> when it did.

## Why this chapter matters

The most practically valuable chapter in Géron's second half. Normalization, residual thinking,
adaptive optimizers, learning-rate schedules, and transfer learning are all *directly* present in
LLM training. When Raschka's Appendix D adds warmup and cosine decay to the GPT training loop,
this is the chapter that tells you why.

## Core concepts

### The vanishing/exploding gradient problem

Gradients are products of many terms as they flow backwards. Multiply many numbers < 1 and you get
~0 (**vanishing** — lower layers never learn). Multiply many > 1 and you get ∞ (**exploding** —
divergence, `nan`).

Glorot & Bengio's 2010 diagnosis: the old default of sigmoid activations with $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$
initialization made each layer's output variance *larger* than its input's, until saturation. At
saturation, sigmoid's derivative is ≈ 0, so nothing flows back.

**Fix 1 — initialization.** Keep the variance of activations and gradients roughly constant across
layers by scaling the initial weights to fan-in/fan-out:

| Init | Use with |
|---|---|
| **Glorot** (Xavier) | tanh, sigmoid, softmax, linear |
| **He** | ReLU and variants |
| **LeCun** | SELU |

**Fix 2 — better activations.** ReLU does not saturate for positive inputs, but suffers **dying
ReLUs**: a neuron whose weighted sum stays negative outputs 0 forever, and gradient 0 means it
never recovers.

| Activation | Fixes | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Leaky ReLU** | Dying ReLU (small slope α for z<0) | Essentially none |
| **ELU** | Dying ReLU, smoother, mean nearer 0 | Slower (exp) |
| **SELU** | Self-normalizes — layers keep mean 0, std 1 | Strict conditions |

SELU's conditions are demanding: sequential architecture only, standardized inputs, LeCun normal
init, no regularization that breaks self-normalization. When it applies it is excellent.

`[dated]` **GELU** and **SwiGLU** postdate this edition and are what transformers actually use.
GELU is a smooth ReLU-like curve; you will implement it in
[R4](../../03-build-llm-from-scratch-raschka/notes/ch04.md). The reasoning here still applies.

**Fix 3 — Batch Normalization.** Normalize each layer's inputs to zero mean and unit variance
over the mini-batch, then rescale with two *learned* parameters γ (scale) and β (shift):

$$\hat{x} = \frac{x - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}}, \qquad z = \gamma \hat{x} + \beta$$

The learned γ and β are the point — the network can undo the normalization if it needs to, so
you lose no expressiveness. BN also acts as a regularizer (mini-batch noise) and lets you use
much larger learning rates. At inference, running averages of μ and σ replace batch statistics —
which is why BN behaves differently in `train` vs `eval` mode, a bug source in every framework.

`[dated]` Transformers use **Layer Normalization** instead — normalizing across features within
one example rather than across the batch. That makes it independent of batch size and of other
sequences in the batch, which matters for variable-length text and for inference with batch
size 1. You will implement LayerNorm in [R4](../../03-build-llm-from-scratch-raschka/notes/ch04.md).

**Fix 4 — Gradient clipping.** Cap gradients at a threshold. Standard in RNN and LLM training;
`clip_grad_norm_` appears in essentially every modern training loop.

### Transfer learning

Reuse the lower layers of a trained network. The recipe:

1. Freeze the reused layers.
2. Train the new output layer to convergence.
3. Unfreeze the top one or two reused layers, **lower the learning rate**, continue.

The lower the layer, the more generic its features (edges, textures) and the more worth keeping.

Géron is refreshingly blunt: transfer learning **does not work well for small dense networks**,
because they learn few and highly specific patterns. It shines for CNNs, which learn genuinely
generic low-level features.

> This is the direct ancestor of LLM fine-tuning. "Freeze the base, train a head" is
> [R6](../../03-build-llm-from-scratch-raschka/notes/ch06.md). "Lower learning rate when
> unfreezing" is standard fine-tuning practice. LoRA
> ([H7](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch07.md)) is a cleverer answer to the same question:
> how do I adapt a big pretrained model without destroying it or paying full price?

**Unsupervised pretraining** — train on unlabeled data first, then fine-tune on your few labels —
is presented here as a niche technique. It became the entire paradigm.

### Faster optimizers

All improve on plain gradient descent, and all are worth knowing by name because you will select
among them:

| Optimizer | Idea | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Momentum** | Accumulate a velocity vector | Rolls through plateaus and small local minima |
| **Nesterov** | Measure the gradient *ahead* along the momentum | Slightly better, ~free |
| **AdaGrad** | Scale down each dimension by accumulated squared gradients | **Stops too early** on deep nets |
| **RMSProp** | AdaGrad with exponential decay | Fixes AdaGrad's problem |
| **Adam** | Momentum + RMSProp | The default |
| **AdaMax / Nadam** | Adam variants | Occasionally better |

`[dated]` **AdamW** — Adam with *decoupled* weight decay — is now the standard for transformers.
Adam's L2 penalty interacts badly with its adaptive scaling; AdamW separates them. Every LLM you
have used was trained with AdamW.

Adaptive methods sometimes generalize slightly worse than SGD+Nesterov. Géron's advice — if
results disappoint, try plain momentum — remains good.

### Learning-rate schedules

Better than any fixed rate. Power scheduling, exponential, piecewise constant, performance-based,
and **1cycle** (Smith) — which ramps *up* then down and can dramatically shorten training.

`[dated]` LLM training standardizes on **linear warmup then cosine decay**. Warmup exists because
Adam's variance estimates are unreliable in the first few hundred steps; starting at full learning
rate then can destabilize training permanently. This is exactly Raschka's Appendix D.

### Regularization for deep nets

- **ℓ1/ℓ2** — as in [G4](ch04.md), via `kernel_regularizer`.
- **Dropout** — drop each neuron with probability *p* at each training step. Neurons cannot
  co-adapt. Typically 10–50%. **Only active during training**; at test time weights are scaled by
  the keep probability. If your model overfits, raise *p*.
- **Monte Carlo Dropout** — keep dropout on at inference, average many predictions. You get a
  better estimate *and* an uncertainty measure, with no retraining. Underused.
- **Max-norm** — constrain each neuron's weight vector norm.

### Géron's default configuration

Worth memorizing as a starting point:

| Setting | Default | Self-normalizing variant |
|---|---|---|
| Initializer | He | LeCun normal |
| Activation | ELU | SELU |
| Normalization | None (shallow) / BN (deep) | None |
| Regularization | Early stopping (+ ℓ2) | Alpha dropout |
| Optimizer | Momentum / RMSProp / Nadam | same |
| LR schedule | 1cycle | same |

## Key code

```python
# BatchNorm before the activation (Géron discusses both orderings)
model = keras.Sequential([
    keras.layers.Dense(300, kernel_initializer="he_normal", use_bias=False),
    keras.layers.BatchNormalization(),
    keras.layers.Activation("elu"),
    keras.layers.Dropout(rate=0.2),
    keras.layers.Dense(10, activation="softmax"),
])

optimizer = keras.optimizers.Nadam(learning_rate=1e-3, clipnorm=1.0)

# Transfer learning: freeze, train the head, then unfreeze at a LOWER lr
for layer in base_model.layers[:-1]:
    layer.trainable = False
model.compile(optimizer=keras.optimizers.SGD(learning_rate=1e-3), ...)
model.fit(...)                      # head only

for layer in base_model.layers[:-1]:
    layer.trainable = True
model.compile(optimizer=keras.optimizers.SGD(learning_rate=1e-4), ...)  # 10x lower
model.fit(...)
```

## Gotchas

- **Not re-compiling after changing `trainable`.** In Keras the change does not take effect until
  you recompile. A classic silent failure — you think you froze layers and you did not.
- **Same learning rate after unfreezing.** You will destroy the pretrained weights in a few
  steps. Drop it by ~10×.
- **`use_bias=True` before BatchNorm.** BN has its own β shift, so the preceding bias is
  redundant. Harmless but wasteful.
- **Forgetting BN behaves differently at train vs inference.** Small batches give noisy
  statistics; batch size 1 breaks it. LayerNorm avoids this entirely.
- **Dropout at inference.** Frameworks handle it, but hand-rolled loops often forget `model.eval()`
  / `training=False`.
- **Dropout rate copied from a paper.** It is dataset- and architecture-dependent. Tune it.
- **Using AdaGrad on a deep network.** Its accumulated denominator only grows, so the effective
  learning rate decays to zero before convergence. Use RMSProp or Adam.

## Exercises

**Understand**
1. Why do gradients vanish, and how does initialization address it?
2. What are the two *learned* parameters in batch norm, and why are they necessary?
3. Why does BN behave differently at training and inference time?
4. Why does transfer learning work well for CNNs and poorly for small dense networks?
5. What problem does learning-rate warmup solve?

**Build**
1. Deep net on CIFAR-10 (Géron ex. 8): 20 hidden layers of 100 neurons, He init, ELU, Nadam,
   early stopping. Then add BN and compare convergence speed and final accuracy.
2. Swap in SELU with the full self-normalizing setup. Compare.
3. Add alpha dropout, then apply **MC Dropout** at inference and see whether averaged predictions
   beat the single deterministic one.
4. Implement 1cycle scheduling manually and compare against a constant learning rate.
5. Transfer learning: train on MNIST digits 0–4, then transfer to 5–9 with only 100 labeled
   examples (ex. 9–10). Measure the gain over training from scratch.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: Why do gradients vanish in deep networks?
A: Backprop multiplies many terms; if they are consistently < 1 the product approaches zero and lower layers stop learning. Historically caused by saturating sigmoid activations plus N(0,1) initialization, which grew activation variance until neurons saturated where the derivative is ~0.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Which initialization for which activation?
A: Glorot/Xavier for tanh, sigmoid, softmax, and linear. He for ReLU and its variants. LeCun for SELU.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What does batch normalization do, and what are γ and β?
A: It normalizes each layer's inputs to zero mean and unit variance over the mini-batch, then rescales with learned γ (scale) and β (shift). The learned parameters mean the network can undo the normalization, so no expressiveness is lost.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why do transformers use LayerNorm instead of BatchNorm?
A: LayerNorm normalizes across features within a single example, so it is independent of batch size and of other sequences in the batch. That matters for variable-length text and for inference at batch size 1, where BatchNorm's batch statistics are unusable.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is a dying ReLU and how do you fix it?
A: A neuron whose weighted sum is always negative outputs 0 and receives gradient 0, so it never recovers. Fix with Leaky ReLU, ELU, or SELU, which have a nonzero slope for negative inputs.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the transfer-learning recipe?
A: Freeze the reused layers, train the new output layer to convergence, then unfreeze the top one or two reused layers and continue with a ~10× lower learning rate. In Keras you must recompile after changing `trainable`.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does LLM training use learning-rate warmup?
A: Adam's moment estimates are unreliable during the first few hundred steps; applying the full learning rate then can destabilize training permanently. Warmup ramps the rate up gradually, then cosine decay anneals it.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is AdamW and why did it replace Adam for transformers?
A: Adam with decoupled weight decay. Adam's L2 penalty interacts badly with its per-parameter adaptive scaling, so the effective regularization varies by parameter. AdamW applies decay separately from the adaptive step.
<!-- /card -->
