# G5 — Support Vector Machines

> **Thesis:** Instead of finding *a* separating boundary, find the one with the widest possible
> margin — and then use the kernel trick to do it in a space you never actually compute.

## Why this chapter matters

Honestly? Less than the chapters around it, for an LLM-focused path. SVMs were the state of the
art before deep learning and are now a strong baseline for small, clean, medium-dimensional
datasets. **Skim it.**

Two ideas do earn their keep and stay with you:

1. **Large-margin thinking** — preferring the solution with the most room for error. This
   reappears in contrastive embedding training
   ([A10](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch10.md)), where triplet loss is a margin loss.
2. **The kernel trick** — computing similarity in a high-dimensional space without ever
   materializing the coordinates. A genuinely beautiful piece of mathematics and useful mental
   furniture for thinking about embeddings.

## Core concepts

### Large margin classification

An SVM fits the widest possible "street" between classes. Only the instances on the edge — the
**support vectors** — determine the boundary; all other points could move freely without changing
anything. That is why SVMs are memory-efficient at prediction time.

**Hard margin** requires perfect linear separability and is wrecked by a single outlier.
**Soft margin** allows margin violations, controlled by **C**:

- **Low C** → wide street, more violations, more regularized (may underfit)
- **High C** → narrow street, few violations, less regularized (may overfit)

If your SVM overfits, **lower C**. That is the inverse of most regularization knobs, and it
catches people out constantly.

**SVMs are extremely sensitive to feature scaling** — unscaled, the street is dominated by the
largest-magnitude feature.

### Nonlinear SVMs and the kernel trick

Add polynomial features and a linear SVM can handle curves. But high degrees mean a combinatorial
explosion of features.

The **kernel trick** avoids materializing them. A kernel $K(a,b)$ computes the dot product *as
if* you had projected into a high-dimensional space:

| Kernel | Formula | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Linear | $a^\top b$ | Text, many features |
| Polynomial | $(\gamma a^\top b + r)^d$ | Moderate nonlinearity |
| **Gaussian RBF** | $\exp(-\gamma \lVert a-b \rVert^2)$ | The default nonlinear choice |
| Sigmoid | $\tanh(\gamma a^\top b + r)$ | Rarely |

The RBF kernel is a **similarity function**: 1 when identical, decaying to 0 with distance.
`gamma` controls how fast it decays — **high gamma = narrow bell = wigglier boundary = overfit**.
So `gamma` and `C` are both regularization knobs; lower both if you are overfitting.

Géron's advice: try `LinearSVC` first (fastest), then RBF `SVC` if the dataset is not too large,
then others.

### Computational complexity — the reason SVMs lost

| Class | Complexity | Out-of-core | Kernel |
|---|---|---|---|
| `LinearSVC` | $O(m \times n)$ | No | No |
| `SGDClassifier` | $O(m \times n)$ | **Yes** | No |
| `SVC` | $O(m^2 n)$ to $O(m^3 n)$ | No | **Yes** |

That `SVC` row is the whole story. Quadratic-to-cubic in the number of *instances* means kernel
SVMs simply do not go to millions of rows. Neural networks scale linearly with data, and data
kept growing. That is why the field moved.

### SVM regression

Invert the objective: instead of the widest street with fewest points inside, fit the street so
it contains **as many** points as possible within a tolerance ε. `LinearSVR` / `SVR`.

## Key code

```python
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import LinearSVC, SVC

# Scaling is mandatory, so always use a pipeline.
linear = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LinearSVC(C=1, loss="hinge"))

# C and gamma are BOTH regularization knobs — decrease both to fight overfitting.
rbf = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="rbf", gamma=5, C=0.001))
```

## Gotchas

- **Not scaling.** The single biggest SVM mistake. Always pipeline with `StandardScaler`.
- **Raising C to fix overfitting.** Backwards — lower it.
- **Using `SVC` on a large dataset.** Superlinear in instances; it will hang. Use `LinearSVC`
  or `SGDClassifier` past ~100k rows.
- **Expecting probabilities.** SVMs output distances, not probabilities. `probability=True`
  works but runs an expensive internal cross-validated calibration.
- **`LinearSVC` regularizes the bias.** Center your data first (`StandardScaler` does this).
- **`LinearSVC(loss="hinge")`** is not the default; the default is squared hinge.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: What is a **support vector**?
A: A training instance on or inside the margin street. Only these determine the decision boundary — all other instances could be moved (without crossing the margin) and the model would not change.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Your SVM is overfitting. Which way do you move `C` and `gamma`?
A: **Decrease both.** Lower C widens the street and allows more violations; lower gamma widens the RBF bell and smooths the boundary. Both are regularization knobs where *smaller means more regularized*.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the kernel trick?
A: A way to compute the dot product of two instances *as if* they had been projected into a very high-dimensional feature space, without ever computing that projection. It gives you the expressiveness of high-degree features at the cost of a similarity function.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why did kernel SVMs lose to neural networks on large datasets?
A: `SVC` training is O(m²n) to O(m³n) in the number of instances, so it does not scale past ~100k rows and cannot train out-of-core. Neural networks train with mini-batch SGD, which is linear in data and streams.
<!-- /card -->
