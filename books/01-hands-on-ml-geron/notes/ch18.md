# G18 — Reinforcement Learning

> **Thesis:** When you cannot say what the right output is, but you can say how good an outcome
> was, you optimize a policy against a reward — and then spend all your effort on the fact that
> the reward arrives late and tells you almost nothing about which action caused it.

## Why this chapter matters

Marked **Context**, and you can skip the details — but do not skip the *concepts*, because
**RLHF is the reason modern LLMs are usable**, and RLHF is this chapter applied to text.

The mapping is exact:

| RL concept | RLHF equivalent |
|---|---|
| Agent | The language model |
| Action | Emitting a token |
| Policy π(a\|s) | The model's next-token distribution |
| Environment | The conversation |
| Reward | A reward model trained on human preferences |
| Credit assignment | Which token in a 500-token answer made it good? |
| Policy gradient | PPO (and DPO's reformulation) |

When [H2](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch02.md) discusses preference finetuning, RLHF, and
DPO, this chapter is the vocabulary.

## Core concepts

### The setup and the hard part

An **agent** observes a **state**, takes an **action** in an **environment**, and receives a
**reward**. The goal is to maximize expected cumulative discounted reward. A **policy** π maps
states to actions (usually to a distribution over actions).

**The credit assignment problem** is the whole difficulty. You win the game after 200 moves. Which
moves were good? The reward is delayed and aggregate; the actions are many and individual.

The standard tool is the **discount factor γ**. A reward *n* steps later counts for γⁿ. γ ≈ 0.95
means ~13 steps of meaningful lookahead; γ ≈ 0.99 means ~69. Low γ is myopic; high γ makes credit
assignment harder and training noisier.

**Return** — the discounted sum of future rewards from a step onward — is what an action is
actually scored on.

> In RLHF, credit assignment is brutal: a single scalar reward for a whole response must be
> attributed across hundreds of token-level decisions. This is a large part of why RLHF is
> finicky, and a large part of why **DPO** — which sidesteps the RL loop entirely by optimizing a
> preference objective directly — became popular.

### Policy gradients and REINFORCE

Optimize the policy's parameters directly by following the gradient of expected reward:

1. Play several episodes, recording gradients at each step but **not applying them**.
2. Compute each action's discounted return.
3. **Normalize** returns across the batch (subtract mean, divide by std) → the **advantage**.
   Actions better than average get positive advantage, worse get negative.
4. Multiply each stored gradient by its advantage and apply.

Step 3 is the important one. Without a baseline, every action in a winning episode is reinforced,
including the bad ones. Normalizing makes the signal *relative*, which massively reduces variance.

> PPO — the algorithm in classic RLHF — is a refinement of this with a clipped objective that
> prevents any single update from moving the policy too far. The "too far" concern is the same
> intuition as lowering the learning rate when unfreezing in [G11](ch11.md): you have something
> good and you do not want to destroy it. In RLHF a KL penalty against the reference model does
> this job explicitly.

### Markov decision processes and value iteration

An **MDP** is the formal model: states, actions, transition probabilities, rewards, and the
**Markov property** (the future depends only on the present state, not the history).

**Bellman optimality equation** — the value of a state is the immediate reward plus the discounted
value of the best next state. Iterating it converges to the optimal values.

**Q-values** *Q(s,a)* — the value of taking action *a* in state *s* and behaving optimally
afterwards. Given optimal Q-values, the optimal policy is just `argmax_a Q(s,a)`.

**Q-Learning** learns Q-values from experience without knowing the transition probabilities. It is
**off-policy** — it learns the optimal policy while behaving with a different, exploratory one.

### Exploration vs exploitation

You cannot learn about actions you never take. **ε-greedy**: act randomly with probability ε,
greedily otherwise, and decay ε over training.

This tension has a direct analogue in generation: **temperature** ([G16](ch16.md)) is the
exploration knob. Temperature 0 is pure exploitation of the model's current beliefs. Sampling at
higher temperature explores — which is exactly what **self-consistency** and best-of-*n* sampling
exploit ([A6](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch06.md)).

### Deep Q-Networks and the tricks that make them work

Approximate Q with a neural network. Naively this is very unstable, and the fixes are instructive
because variants of them recur throughout deep learning:

| Trick | Problem it solves |
|---|---|
| **Replay buffer** | Consecutive experiences are correlated; SGD assumes IID. Sample randomly from a buffer. |
| **Target network** | The target moves as you update, so you chase yourself. Freeze a copy, update it periodically. |
| **Double DQN** | Q-learning systematically overestimates values (max of noisy estimates is biased upward). Decouple action selection from evaluation. |
| **Dueling DQN** | Separate state value from action advantage. |
| **Prioritized replay** | Sample surprising transitions more often. |

The "max of noisy estimates is biased upward" point in Double DQN generalizes far beyond RL. It is
the same statistical fact behind **best-of-n selection overestimating quality** when you pick the
best according to a noisy judge — a real hazard in LLM evaluation
([H3](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch03.md)).

`[dated]` The chapter uses TF-Agents. **Gymnasium** (the maintained fork of OpenAI Gym) plus
**Stable-Baselines3** is the current standard; **TRL** is what you would actually use for RLHF.

## Key code

```python
# REINFORCE: the essential shape. Note that gradients are stored, not applied,
# until the episode's returns are known.
def play_one_step(env, obs, model, loss_fn):
    with tf.GradientTape() as tape:
        left_proba = model(obs[np.newaxis])
        action = (tf.random.uniform([1, 1]) > left_proba)     # sample, don't argmax — explore
        y_target = tf.constant([[1.]]) - tf.cast(action, tf.float32)
        loss = tf.reduce_mean(loss_fn(y_target, left_proba))
    grads = tape.gradient(loss, model.trainable_variables)
    obs, reward, done, info = env.step(int(action))
    return obs, reward, done, grads

def discount_and_normalize(all_rewards, gamma):
    discounted = [discount(r, gamma) for r in all_rewards]
    flat = np.concatenate(discounted)
    mean, std = flat.mean(), flat.std()
    # The baseline: advantage = return - mean. Without it, every action in a
    # winning episode gets reinforced, including the bad ones.
    return [(d - mean) / std for d in discounted]
```

## Gotchas

- **Not normalizing returns.** Every action in a good episode gets reinforced. High variance,
  slow or failed learning.
- **γ too high or too low.** Low γ is myopic; high γ makes credit assignment harder and training
  noisier. It is a real hyperparameter, not a formality.
- **No exploration.** A purely greedy policy locks in early and never discovers better actions.
- **Training DQN without a replay buffer or target network.** It will diverge. These are not
  optimizations, they are requirements.
- **Reward hacking.** The agent optimizes the reward you *wrote*, not the one you *meant*. This is
  the single most important RL lesson for LLM work — reward models are proxies, and optimizing
  hard against a proxy exploits its flaws.
- **Reading the reward curve as progress.** RL training is famously noisy across seeds. Run
  several.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the credit assignment problem?
A: Rewards are delayed and aggregate, so it is unclear which of the many actions taken deserve credit. Discounting (γ) and advantage estimation are the standard partial answers. In RLHF, one scalar reward must be attributed across hundreds of token decisions.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why normalize returns in policy gradient methods?
A: Without a baseline, every action in a successful episode is reinforced — including the bad ones. Subtracting the mean makes the signal *relative* (an advantage), so only better-than-average actions get positive weight, drastically reducing variance.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Map RL vocabulary onto RLHF.
A: Agent = the LLM; action = emitting a token; policy = the next-token distribution; environment = the conversation; reward = a reward model trained on human preferences; policy gradient = PPO. DPO skips the RL loop by optimizing a preference objective directly.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does a DQN need a replay buffer and a target network?
A: Consecutive experiences are highly correlated, violating SGD's IID assumption — the replay buffer decorrelates them. And the regression target moves as you update the network, so you chase your own tail — a periodically-frozen target network stabilizes it.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is reward hacking, and why does it matter for LLMs?
A: The agent optimizes the reward you specified rather than the outcome you intended, exploiting flaws in the proxy. Reward models in RLHF are proxies for human preference; optimizing hard against them finds their failure modes — which is why a KL penalty to the reference model is used.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What statistical bias does Double DQN correct, and where else does it appear?
A: Taking the max over noisy estimates is biased upward, so plain Q-learning overestimates values. The same bias makes best-of-n selection overestimate quality when the selector is a noisy judge — a real hazard in LLM evaluation.
