# G14 — Deep Computer Vision Using CNNs

> **Thesis:** A CNN is a dense network with two constraints baked in — locality and weight
> sharing — and those constraints are exactly the prior knowledge that makes vision tractable.

## Why this chapter matters

Beyond vision itself, three ideas here are load-bearing for the LLM half of the roadmap:

1. **Inductive bias** — architecture as encoded assumptions. CNNs assume locality and translation
   invariance. Transformers assume almost nothing, which is why they need vastly more data and
   why they win when they have it.
2. **Residual connections** — ResNet's skip connections are *the* reason very deep networks train
   at all, and the transformer block is built on the same trick
   ([R4](../../03-build-llm-from-scratch-raschka/notes/ch04.md)).
3. **Transfer learning that actually works** — this is the chapter where pretraining pays off
   dramatically, and it is the template for everything in Phase 6.

## Core concepts

### The convolutional layer

Each neuron connects only to a small **receptive field** in the previous layer, and the same
weight matrix (the **filter** / kernel) slides across the whole input.

Two consequences, both huge:

- **Parameter efficiency.** A dense layer on a 100×100 image with 1,000 neurons needs 10 million
  weights. A convolutional layer with 32 filters of 3×3 over 3 channels needs 32 × (3×3×3 + 1) =
  **896**. Four orders of magnitude.
- **Translation equivariance.** A feature detector learned in one location works everywhere,
  because it is the same weights.

Key hyperparameters:

- **Kernel size** — 3×3 dominates. Two stacked 3×3 layers have the same receptive field as one
  5×5 with fewer parameters and an extra nonlinearity.
- **Stride** — step size. Stride 2 halves spatial dimensions.
- **Padding** — `"same"` preserves size (zero-pads), `"valid"` shrinks.
- **Filters** — the number of feature maps. Conventionally doubles as spatial size halves.

**Memory is the practical constraint**, and it surprises people: a single 150×100 layer with 200
5×5 filters on RGB needs ~12 MB *per instance* for activations. At batch size 100 that is 1.2 GB —
and backprop keeps every layer's activations for the backward pass
([G10](ch10.md)). Out-of-memory during training almost always means batch size.

### Pooling

Downsample to reduce computation, memory, and parameter count, and to introduce a small amount of
**translation invariance** (not just equivariance). Max pooling is standard; it is destructive by
design — a 2×2 max pool discards 75% of the input.

**Global average pooling** reduces each feature map to a single number. Brutally lossy but very
effective as the final layer before classification, and it removes the big flatten-then-dense
parameter block.

`[dated]` Modern architectures increasingly replace pooling with strided convolutions.

### The canonical architecture, and the classic mistake

Stack convolutions, pool periodically, double the filter count as spatial resolution halves, then
flatten and classify.

The mistake Géron calls out: **do not use large kernels in the first layer.** A single 5×5 has
more parameters and less expressive power than two stacked 3×3s. The exception is the very first
layer on a large image, where a big stride is used to cut resolution cheaply.

### The architecture lineage

| Year | Model | The idea it contributed |
|---|---|---|
| 1998 | LeNet-5 | The template |
| 2012 | AlexNet | Depth + GPUs + dropout. **The moment deep learning arrived.** |
| 2014 | GoogLeNet | Inception modules; 1×1 convolutions as channel-wise bottlenecks |
| 2014 | VGGNet | Simplicity — just 3×3 convolutions, stacked deep |
| **2015** | **ResNet** | **Residual connections** — 152 layers trainable |
| 2016+ | Xception, SENet | Depthwise-separable convs; channel attention |

**ResNet deserves its own paragraph.** Instead of learning a target function *h(x)*, a residual
block learns *f(x) = h(x) − x*, and the output is *f(x) + x*. The skip connection means:

- Gradients have a **direct path** backwards, so they cannot vanish through the block.
- An untrained block starts as the **identity function**, so adding depth never hurts at
  initialization. The network can effectively choose its own depth.

> This is precisely the transformer block's structure: `x = x + attention(norm(x))`, then
> `x = x + ffn(norm(x))`. The "residual stream" that interpretability researchers talk about is
> the `x` being carried through those additions. When you write it in
> [R4](../../03-build-llm-from-scratch-raschka/notes/ch04.md), you are writing ResNet's idea.

**SENet's squeeze-and-excitation** block is worth noting too: it learns per-channel importance
weights and rescales the feature maps. That is **attention over channels** — the same "learn what
to focus on" idea that attention applies over positions.

### Transfer learning, where it shines

The recipe from [G11](ch11.md), and here it works spectacularly:

1. Load a pretrained model (ImageNet weights), drop the top.
2. Add your classification head.
3. Freeze the base, train the head for a few epochs.
4. Unfreeze the top layers, drop the learning rate ~10×, continue.

Géron's flowers example reaches high accuracy on a few thousand images — training from scratch
would need orders of magnitude more. **Preprocess your inputs exactly as the pretrained model
expects** (`keras.applications.xception.preprocess_input`); getting this wrong is a common silent
failure.

### Beyond classification

- **Localization** — predict a bounding box; regression with MSE. Better metric: **IoU**.
- **Object detection** — fully convolutional networks, YOLO, SSD. Non-max suppression to
  deduplicate. Metric: **mAP**.
- **Semantic segmentation** — per-pixel classification, using transposed convolutions to upsample
  and skip connections to recover spatial detail lost to pooling.

## Key code

```python
from tensorflow import keras

# Two 3x3 convs > one 5x5: fewer params, more nonlinearity
model = keras.Sequential([
    keras.layers.Conv2D(64, 7, activation="relu", padding="same", input_shape=[224, 224, 3]),
    keras.layers.MaxPooling2D(2),
    keras.layers.Conv2D(128, 3, activation="relu", padding="same"),
    keras.layers.Conv2D(128, 3, activation="relu", padding="same"),
    keras.layers.MaxPooling2D(2),
    keras.layers.GlobalAveragePooling2D(),
    keras.layers.Dense(10, activation="softmax"),
])

# Transfer learning
base = keras.applications.Xception(weights="imagenet", include_top=False)
avg = keras.layers.GlobalAveragePooling2D()(base.output)
output = keras.layers.Dense(n_classes, activation="softmax")(avg)
model = keras.Model(inputs=base.input, outputs=output)

for layer in base.layers:
    layer.trainable = False
model.compile(optimizer=keras.optimizers.SGD(learning_rate=0.2, momentum=0.9), ...)
model.fit(train_set, epochs=5)

for layer in base.layers:
    layer.trainable = True
model.compile(optimizer=keras.optimizers.SGD(learning_rate=0.01, momentum=0.9), ...)  # lower!
model.fit(train_set, epochs=40)
```

## Gotchas

- **Out of memory.** Activation memory, not parameters. Reduce batch size first.
- **Large kernels in early layers.** Two 3×3s beat one 5×5 on both parameters and expressiveness.
- **Wrong preprocessing for a pretrained model.** Each expects a specific input scaling
  (`[-1,1]`, `[0,1]`, ImageNet mean-subtracted). Use the model's own `preprocess_input`.
- **Not lowering the learning rate when unfreezing.** You will wipe out the pretrained features.
- **Forgetting to recompile after `trainable` changes.** Keras requires it.
- **Using `"valid"` padding without noticing the shrink.** Deep stacks silently shrink to nothing.
- **Data augmentation applied to the validation set.** Augment training only.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: What two constraints does a CNN impose on a dense layer, and what do they buy?
A: **Locality** (each neuron sees only a small receptive field) and **weight sharing** (the same filter slides everywhere). They buy an enormous reduction in parameters and translation equivariance.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why prefer two stacked 3×3 convolutions over one 5×5?
A: Same receptive field, fewer parameters (2×9=18 vs 25 per channel pair), and an extra nonlinearity between them, so more expressive power for less cost.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What problem do residual (skip) connections solve, and how?
A: Vanishing gradients in very deep networks. The block learns f(x) = h(x) − x and outputs f(x) + x, giving gradients a direct path backwards and making an untrained block an identity function — so adding depth never hurts at initialization.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Where do residual connections appear in a transformer?
A: The whole block: `x = x + attention(norm(x))` then `x = x + ffn(norm(x))`. The `x` carried through those additions is what interpretability work calls the **residual stream**.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why does CNN training run out of memory, and what do you change first?
A: Activation memory, not weights — backprop caches every layer's activations for the backward pass, and it scales with batch size. Reduce the batch size first.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is inductive bias, with CNNs and transformers as the contrast?
A: The assumptions an architecture encodes. CNNs assume locality and translation invariance, so they learn efficiently from little data on images. Transformers assume almost nothing, so they need far more data — and surpass CNNs once they have it.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is a 1×1 convolution for?
A: It has no spatial extent, so it acts purely across channels — a learned linear mixing of feature maps, typically used as a cheap dimensionality bottleneck (GoogLeNet, ResNet bottleneck blocks).
<!-- /card -->
