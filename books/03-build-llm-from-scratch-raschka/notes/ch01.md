# R1 — Understanding Large Language Models

> **Thesis:** An LLM is a next-token predictor trained on enormous text, and the three-stage plan
> for building one — data, architecture, training — is small enough to fit in your head.

## Why this chapter matters

It is the map for the next six chapters. Short, and worth reading carefully once, because the
staging Raschka lays out here is what makes the rest of the book feel inevitable rather than
arbitrary.

If you have read [A1](../../02-hands-on-llms-alammar/notes/ch01.md), much of the content is
familiar. Read this one for the **plan**, not the background.

## Core concepts

### What "large" means

Large in parameters (billions), in training data (trillions of tokens), and in the resulting
generality. The parameter count matters less than people think — post-training quality and data
quality routinely beat raw size ([H2](../../04-ai-engineering-huyen/notes/ch02.md)).

### The two-stage recipe

**Stage 1 — Pretraining.** Self-supervised next-token prediction on a huge unlabeled corpus.
Expensive (GPT-3 was estimated at ~$4.6M in compute), done once. Produces a **base**/foundation
model that completes text.

**Stage 2 — Fine-tuning.** Comparatively cheap, on curated data. Two kinds, and the book does
both:
- **Instruction fine-tuning** — (instruction, response) pairs → chapter 7
- **Classification fine-tuning** — (text, label) pairs → chapter 6

The label-free nature of stage 1 is the whole trick. The next word *is* the label
([G1](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch01.md) — self-supervision), so the internet becomes a
training set.

### Why the transformer

Raschka's framing: the transformer's decisive property is that **training parallelizes across
positions**. RNNs process sequentially ([G15](../../01-hands-on-ml-geron/notes/ch15.md)); the
transformer computes all positions in one matrix multiplication. That converts a sequential
problem into a parallel one — and parallel problems ride hardware improvements.

**GPT is decoder-only.** The original transformer had an encoder and a decoder for translation.
GPT keeps only the decoder with causal masking. Simpler, and it turned out that next-token
prediction at scale subsumes the tasks the encoder was for.

### Emergence

Capabilities like few-shot learning and translation were **not trained for**. They appear as
side effects of scale on next-token prediction. Raschka is appropriately measured about this —
"emergence" describes an observation, it does not explain one, and some emergence claims are
artifacts of discontinuous metrics.

### The plan

Raschka's three stages, which map exactly onto the chapters:

| Stage | Chapters | You build |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Data & architecture** | 2–4 | Tokenizer, embeddings, attention, the GPT class |
| **2. Pretraining** | 5 | Training loop, evaluation, weight loading |
| **3. Fine-tuning** | 6–7 | Classifier, then instruction-follower |

Target: **GPT-2 small — 124M parameters, 12 layers, 12 heads, 768 dimensions.** Small enough to
train on a laptop, large enough to be a real model. And OpenAI released the weights, so at the
end of chapter 5 you can load them into *your* architecture — which is the proof your
implementation is correct.

## Gotchas

- **Reading instead of typing.** The entire value is in writing the code. Cloning the repo and
  running it teaches you almost nothing.
- **Skipping Appendix A.** If `nn.Module`, `forward`, and `backward` are not automatic, chapter 3
  will be about PyTorch rather than about attention.
- **Assuming you need a GPU.** You do not. Chapters 2–4 are CPU work; chapter 5's pretraining run
  is deliberately small.
- **Expecting your model to be good.** A 124M model trained on a short story generates barely
  coherent text. That is the expected result. The point is that you built it.

## Exercises

**Understand**
1. What makes pretraining self-supervised, and why does that matter economically?
2. Why is training parallelism the transformer's decisive advantage over RNNs?
3. Why is GPT decoder-only when the original transformer had both halves?
4. What are the three stages of the book, and what do you build in each?

**Build**
1. Set up your environment and confirm PyTorch works.
2. Work through Appendix A if there is any doubt. Write a training loop for a toy regression from
   memory — no reference.
3. Write down the GPT-2 small configuration (124M, 12 layers, 12 heads, 768 dims, 1024 context,
   50257 vocab). You will type it many times.

## Flashcards

<!-- card -->
Q: What are the two stages of building an LLM?
A: Pretraining — self-supervised next-token prediction on a huge unlabeled corpus, expensive and done once, producing a base model. Then fine-tuning — cheap, on curated labeled data, producing an instruction-follower or a classifier.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: Why is next-token prediction self-supervised?
A: The label is the next token in the text itself, so no human annotation is needed. That is what makes internet-scale training corpora usable and is the economic basis of the whole field.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What is the transformer's decisive advantage over RNNs?
A: Training parallelizes across positions — all positions compute in one matrix multiplication instead of sequentially. That turns a sequential problem into a parallel one, so it rides hardware improvements.
<!-- /card -->

<!-- card -->
Q: What model does this book build, and why that one?
A: GPT-2 small: 124M parameters, 12 layers, 12 heads, 768 dimensions, 1024 context, 50257 vocabulary. Small enough to train on a laptop, and OpenAI released the weights — so you can load them into your own architecture to prove your implementation is correct.
